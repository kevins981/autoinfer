# Inspiration cards

These cards are offered for inspiration only. Nobody chose them for your team, and they are not a
direction or an assignment.

- **Look at the system yourself first:** read the task, profile, find where the time goes. Then
  come back to the cards. Using none of them is fine.
- **The principle cards are general.** They say nothing about this system; applying them is your
  work.
- **The hardware and software cards are high-level pointers, not a reference.** They may be
  incomplete, out of date or wrong for your case, and each says how it was checked. Do your own
  research and verify any detail before you rely on it.

## General principles

### Skew

**The principle.** Costs are rarely spread evenly. A few cases, items or paths usually account for most of the total. Treating everything the same means designing for an average that hardly occurs: the frequent few get no special care, and the rare many get more than they need.

**The waste.** Uniform treatment of unequal things.

**General directions.**
- **Measure the distribution:** find which few account for most.
- **Make the frequent case cheap:** even if the rare case gets slower.
- **Give the best places to the most used:** the fastest, nearest or shortest.
- **Handle the rare cases apart:** simply, without burdening the common path.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Morse code gives the most common letter a single dot. A supermarket opens an express lane. The most used items in a kitchen sit at hand height.

(card: `15-skew.md`)

### Trade-offs

**The principle.** Improving one thing often worsens another. The usual response is to pick a point between the two. But the two needs seldom apply at the same time, in the same place, to the same things. If they can be separated, each can be met in full.

(This card is not a kind of waste. It is a way out when two goals seem to conflict.)

**The waste.** Settling for a compromise when the two needs could have been separated.

**General directions.**
- **Separate by time:** one way now, the other way later.
- **Separate by place:** one part built one way, another part the other way.
- **Separate by condition:** one way for some cases, the other way for the rest.
- **Separate by scale:** the whole behaves one way, its parts another.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Bifocal glasses give near and far vision in different parts of the lens. A road reverses a lane for the morning and the evening rush. An aircraft wing changes shape between landing and cruising.

(card: `21-trade-offs.md`)

### Unused results

**The principle.** Work has value only through what uses its output. Follow any output forward: if nothing reads it, or only part of it is read, the unread part bought nothing. This happens because the producer usually doesn't know what the consumer will need, so it produces everything to be safe.

**The waste.** Work whose output nobody uses, and the unused part of outputs that are only partly used.

**General directions.**
- **Produce on demand:** wait until something asks for the output.
- **Produce only the part asked for:** let the request say how much is needed.
- **Tell the producer:** pass the consumer's needs back to where the work is done.
- **Stop early:** once the answer is determined, the rest of the work adds nothing.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A restaurant cooks to order instead of cooking the whole menu. A search stops at the first match when one is enough. A map loads only the area on the screen.

(card: `02-unused-results.md`)

### Mismatch with the machine

**The principle.** A machine is not equally good at everything. It has operations and patterns it performs very fast, and others it performs slowly; the difference can be large. The same result can usually be reached through work of different shapes. Speed depends on the fit between the shape of the work and the strengths of the machine, as much as on the amount of work.

**The waste.** Work in a shape the machine does slowly, when an equivalent shape would use what it does fast.

**General directions.**
- **Learn the machine:** what is it fast at, and which of its capabilities are rarely used?
- **Reshape the work:** sizes, order, arrangement, kind of operation, so that it fits.
- **Move work to the part that suits it:** different parts of a machine have different strengths.
- **Ask what is unused:** a capability nobody uses may fit a problem nobody matched it to.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Boxes sized to the truck bed fill it with no gaps. A car is efficient only in the right gear. Multiplying by two is a shift for a binary machine.

(card: `17-mismatch-with-the-machine.md`)

### Unused structure

**The principle.** The cost of work depends on how much the method knows about regularity in its inputs. Data or mathematics with regularity (many zeros, symmetry, sorted order, repeated patterns, few independent directions, operations that may be regrouped) contains less than its size suggests. A method that knows the regularity can do proportionally less.

**The waste.** Work in proportion to the raw size of the problem, when its real content is smaller.

**General directions.**
- **Look for regularity:** measure it; don't assume it is absent.
- **Skip what contributes nothing:** parts known to be zero, unchanged or irrelevant.
- **Do one of a kind:** when parts are symmetric or repeated, do one and reuse it.
- **Use the laws of the mathematics:** regroup, factor or reorder to reach a cheaper equivalent form.
- **Choose a method whose cost follows the content:** not the size.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Sorted order lets you find a name in a phone book in a few steps. Multiplying by a table that is mostly zeros needs only the non-zero entries. Gauss summed 1 to 100 by pairing the ends.

(card: `04-unused-structure.md`)

## This machine: hardware

### Work described ahead of time as a graph

**What it is.** A sequence of kernels and copies, with their dependencies, can be recorded once as a graph and then launched as a unit, any number of times. Parameters of individual nodes can be updated without rebuilding the graph. Graphs can contain conditional nodes (if, while, switch) whose condition is written by a kernel on the GPU, so a decision does not have to go back to the host. A kernel can itself launch a graph.

**What it makes possible.** The cost of issuing many small pieces of work is paid once, and some control flow can stay on the GPU.

**How this was checked.** From the documentation; conditional nodes and device-side launch were not tried on this machine.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: CUDA Graphs.

(card: `15-work-described-ahead-of-time-as-a-graph.md`)

### How the host learns that the GPU is done

**What it is.** When a host thread waits for GPU work, it can spin on a core, yield its core between checks, or sleep until an interrupt arrives. These differ in how fast the thread reacts and how much processor time it burns. Besides waiting, the host can poll an event without blocking, or attach a function that runs when a stream reaches a given point. The device also reports stream operations that write a value to memory or wait until memory holds a value.

**What it makes possible.** The gap between the GPU finishing and the host acting on it is set by the waiting method, and it is paid at every hand-over.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes). The waiting modes are from the documentation.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Asynchronous Execution; driver API: context scheduling flags, stream memory operations.

(card: `16-how-the-host-learns-that-the-gpu-is-done.md`)

### Four one-way lanes

**What it is.** Each PCIe link carries data in both directions at once at full rate, and each GPU has its own link and two copy engines. So there are four independent one-way paths: host to GPU and GPU to host, for each of the two GPUs.

**What it makes possible.** An exchange between the GPUs does not have to be a sequence of turns. Both GPUs can send and receive at the same moment.

**How this was checked.** Follows from the link type and the device's reported copy engines; simultaneous transfers were not measured here.

**Where to read more.** PCI Express specification summaries; CUDA Programming Guide: Asynchronous Execution.

(card: `22-four-one-way-lanes.md`)

### Telling the cache what to keep

**What it is.** A program can influence the second-level cache instead of leaving it to the default policy. Up to 49.5 MiB can be set aside for data marked to persist. A window of memory (up to 128 MiB) is marked per stream or per graph node, with a ratio that says what fraction of accesses in it get the persisting treatment; accesses can also be marked as streaming (use once, evict first). At the instruction level, a load or store can carry an eviction hint (evict first, evict last, do not cache), and there are instructions to prefetch into the cache, to change the priority of a range, and to discard a range without writing it back.

**What it makes possible.** The program knows which data it will need again and which it will not; the default policy does not. These controls let it pass that knowledge on.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes). The hint instructions were assembled for this chip; not run. The set-aside cannot be changed while the multi-process service is in use.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: L2 Cache Control; PTX ISA reference: `prefetch`, `createpolicy`, `applypriority`, `discard`.

(card: `07-telling-the-cache-what-to-keep.md`)

### Two halves of the host

**What it is.** The host is two halves (NUMA nodes), each a processor with its own memory. A core reaches the other half's memory more slowly than its own. Both GPUs attach to the first half (cores 0-11 and 24-35). A thread on the second half, or a buffer placed in the second half's memory, is one step further from both GPUs.

**What it makes possible.** Where a thread runs and where a buffer lives changes the cost of the same operation.

**How this was checked.** Read from `lscpu`, `nvidia-smi topo -m` and the devices' reported NUMA node.

**Where to read more.** `lscpu`; `/sys/devices/system/node/`.

(card: `27-two-halves-of-the-host.md`)

## This machine: software

### Several levels of language for GPU code

**What it is.** GPU code can be written at different levels, and several are installed here. At thread level: CUDA C++ (compiler 13.0) and Numba (Python compiled to the same thing). At block level: Triton, where one program describes a whole thread block and the compiler arranges the threads. At tile level: NVIDIA's CuTe DSL (the building blocks of its matrix-kernel library, usable from Python) and cuTile (kernels written as operations on whole tiles).

**What it makes possible.** The level sets what the author controls and what the compiler decides. The same kernel can be cheap to write at one level and only reach full speed at another, and pieces written at different levels can live in one program.

**How this was checked.** All import in this environment. cuTile's compiler was not run on this chip.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Writing SIMT Kernels, Writing Tile Kernels; the Triton, Numba, CuTe DSL and cuTile documentation.

(card: `01-several-levels-of-language-for-gpu-code.md`)

### Parallel building blocks

**What it is.** A library of general parallel algorithms for the GPU is installed, in C++ and with a Python interface: sort, scan (running totals), reduce, select the items that meet a condition, partition, remove duplicates, histogram, run-length encode, search, merge.

**What it makes possible.** Problems that look inherently sequential, such as compacting a list or grouping equal items, have well-known parallel forms that are already written.

**How this was checked.** The package is installed (CCCL 13.3); not exercised here.

**Where to read more.** CCCL documentation (Thrust, CUB, `cuda.cccl`).

(card: `06-parallel-building-blocks.md`)

### Compiling a whole sequence of operations as one

**What it is.** The framework can trace a model's sequence of operations and hand it to a compiler as one unit, which may fuse operations, drop intermediate results and generate code specialized to the shapes seen. Separately, a sequence of GPU operations can be captured once and replayed as a graph.

**What it makes possible.** Boundaries between operations are a choice of the code as written, not of the computation.

**How this was checked.** Part of the installed framework (torch 2.14); not exercised here.

**Where to read more.** PyTorch documentation: `torch.compile`, `torch.fx`, CUDA graphs.

(card: `04-compiling-a-whole-sequence-of-operations-as-one.md`)

### Ready-made math libraries

**What it is.** NVIDIA's tuned libraries are installed: dense linear algebra (including a lightweight matrix-multiply interface with many formats, and batched forms), sparse linear algebra, matrix multiply with one operand pruned in the fixed pattern the matrix hardware supports, solvers, Fourier transforms, random numbers, the standard deep-learning operations, image and JPEG routines, and reading files directly into GPU memory.

**What it makes possible.** Some of these implement an operation in a way nobody would write by hand, and some expose hardware modes that are otherwise awkward to reach.

**How this was checked.** The packages are installed; which code paths each takes on this chip was not checked.

**Where to read more.** cuBLAS, cuSPARSE, cuSPARSELt, cuSOLVER, cuFFT, cuRAND, cuDNN, NPP, nvJPEG and cuFile documentation.

(card: `05-ready-made-math-libraries.md`)

### Down to the instructions

**What it is.** The compiler's intermediate language (PTX) can be read, written by hand, and placed inline inside C++ code. The assembler reports registers used and spilled per kernel. The final machine instructions can be disassembled from any compiled kernel, including ones from libraries. The profiler can attribute time to single source lines and instructions.

**What it makes possible.** What a kernel really does is visible, and what the higher-level languages do not expose can still be written.

**How this was checked.** The tools are installed (`ptxas`, `nvdisasm`, `cuobjdump`) and the assembler was used to check the hardware cards.

**Where to read more.** PTX ISA reference; CUDA binary utilities documentation.

(card: `03-down-to-the-instructions.md`)
