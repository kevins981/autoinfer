# Inspiration cards

These cards are offered for inspiration only. Nobody chose them for your team, and they are not a
direction or an assignment.

- **Look at the system yourself first:** read the task, profile, find where the time goes. Then
  come back to the cards. Using none of them is fine.
- **The principle cards are general.** They say nothing about this system; applying them is your
  work.
- **The hardware and software cards are high-level pointers, not a reference.** They may be
  incomplete, out of date or wrong for your case, and each says how it was checked. Do your own
  research and verify any detail before you rely on it.

## General principles

### Predictability

**The principle.** Waiting for a result is necessary only if the result can't be predicted. If a guess is usually right, and checking a guess costs less than waiting for the answer, work can go ahead on the guess and be corrected in the rare cases it was wrong. The gain is roughly (chance of being right x time saved) - (chance of being wrong x work wasted). It needs three things: a cheap guess, a cheap check, and a way to undo.

**The waste.** Time spent waiting for an answer that was largely known in advance.

**General directions.**
- **Guess and verify:** proceed on the likely answer; check it when the real one arrives.
- **Prepare ahead:** get ready what is likely to be needed next.
- **Follow several possibilities:** and keep the one that turns out right.
- **Improve the odds or lower the price:** a better guess, a cheaper check, or a cheaper undo.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A processor guesses which way a branch will go and runs ahead. A waiter starts a regular's usual order when they walk in. A disk reads the next blocks before they are asked for.

(card: `08-predictability.md`)

### Holding

**The principle.** Things made or reserved before they are needed occupy capacity while they wait: space, slots, attention. Capacity is finite, so whatever is held ahead of need displaces something else. Holding is usually chosen for safety, to be ready for the worst case. Its cost is hidden, because it shows up as a shortage somewhere else.

**The waste.** Capacity occupied by things that are not in use.

**General directions.**
- **Find what is held:** and how much of it is in use at any moment.
- **Reserve for actual need:** not for the worst case, with a way to get more when needed.
- **Release sooner:** give capacity back as soon as it is no longer used.
- **Share reserves:** among users who are unlikely to need them at the same time.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A factory takes delivery of parts as they are needed instead of filling a warehouse. An airline sells more seats than the plane has, knowing some passengers won't come. An office shares desks among people who are rarely in on the same day.

(card: `20-holding.md`)

### Per-item overhead

**The principle.** The cost of a piece of work often has two parts: one that grows with its size, and a fixed part paid every time regardless of size (setting up, starting, switching, handing over). When items are small, the fixed part dominates. The fixed part belongs to the act of starting, not to the work itself, so several items can share one.

**The waste.** Fixed costs paid more often than necessary.

**General directions.**
- **Group items:** pay the fixed part once for many.
- **Join steps:** let an item pass through several steps without stopping and restarting between them.
- **Stay set up:** keep the preparation between items instead of tearing it down and rebuilding it.
- **Shrink the fixed part:** if it can't be shared, make it cheaper.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A bus carries fifty people with one driver. Laundry is done by the load. Goods cross the ocean in containers, not parcel by parcel.

(card: `10-per-item-overhead.md`)

### Excess guarantees

**The principle.** Every part of a system makes promises to whatever uses it: about order, exactness, freshness, completeness, timing. Each promise costs work to keep. Promises are usually set strong, because strong is safe and the users' real needs weren't known. Often what the users actually rely on is weaker.

The promises a system makes to the outside world are set by its requirements. The promises its parts make to each other are choices.

**The waste.** Work spent keeping a promise nobody relies on.

**General directions.**
- **Ask who relies on it:** for each promise, find what would break without it.
- **Weaken what nobody relies on:** while keeping everything the outside depends on.
- **Keep it only where it matters:** at the points or moments where it is relied on.
- **Keep it more cheaply:** the same promise, delivered by a different mechanism.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Not every letter needs registered mail. A bank need not post each payment the instant it is made. A list need not be fully sorted to find its largest entry.

(card: `18-excess-guarantees.md`)

### Repetition

**The principle.** Any piece of work is a function: it takes inputs and produces an output. The output contains nothing that wasn't already determined by the inputs. So work is only necessary when the inputs are new. If the inputs were seen before, the output already exists somewhere. If part of the inputs is new, only the part of the output that depends on it needs to be made.

This holds for computing a value, and equally for moving or storing one: sending something to a place that already has it is the same waste.

**The waste.** The work done, minus the work the new information required.

**General directions.**
- **Remember:** keep an output so the work isn't done again. This trades memory for work.
- **Share:** produce an output once and let everyone who needs it use the same copy.
- **Update by difference:** when the inputs change a little, compute only what the change affects.
- **Do it ahead:** if the inputs are known long before the output is needed, the work can be done once, early.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Incremental compilation rebuilds only what a change touched. Rsync sends only the parts of a file the other side lacks. A spreadsheet recomputes only the cells that depend on the edited one.

(card: `01-repetition.md`)

## This machine: hardware

### Several processes on one GPU

**What it is.** By default, processes that use the same GPU take turns: the GPU switches between them in time slices, and only one runs at any instant. The multi-process service (MPS) funnels several processes into one shared context so their kernels can run at the same time, and can cap each process's share of the GPU. An ordinary user can start the service for their own processes; its control program is installed. It is not running now, and other people use this machine.

**What it makes possible.** Whether work split across processes overlaps or alternates on the GPU is a choice, not a fixed property.

**How this was checked.** The device reports the default sharing mode and that MPS is off. Starting MPS was not tried.

**Where to read more.** NVIDIA Multi-Process Service documentation.

(card: `14-several-processes-on-one-gpu.md`)

### More than one kernel at a time

**What it is.** A GPU can have up to 128 kernels resident at once. Kernels issued on different streams of one process may run at the same time when the first leaves SMs, registers or scratch memory free; otherwise they run one after another. Streams can be given priorities. The two GPUs are fully independent of each other.

**What it makes possible.** A small kernel does not have to wait behind a large one, and a kernel that cannot use the whole chip need not leave the rest idle.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes).

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Asynchronous Execution.

(card: `12-more-than-one-kernel-at-a-time.md`)

### Host memory

**What it is.** 125 GB of DDR5 memory across the two halves, shared with other users. The processors support 8 memory channels each at up to 4,000 MT/s; how many are populated was not checked. Host memory is slower to read than GPU memory (how much slower was not measured), but there is five times as much of it as one GPU has. A memory-backed file system (`/dev/shm`, 63 GB) lets processes share memory by name.

**What it makes possible.** The host has room to hold things the GPUs cannot, at the cost of the link to reach them.

**How this was checked.** Sizes read from `free` and `df`. Memory speed was not measured.

**Where to read more.** `lscpu`, `dmidecode` output where readable without root.

(card: `28-host-memory.md`)

### Host memory as common ground

**What it is.** Host memory can be pinned (locked in place) and registered with the driver, after which either GPU can copy to and from it directly. The same pinned buffer can serve both GPUs. Memory that already exists, including memory shared between processes, can be registered after the fact, and can be registered read-only. Pinned host memory can also be mapped into a GPU's address space, so a kernel reads or writes it directly, each access crossing the link.

**What it makes possible.** The one place both GPUs can reach is host memory. How that memory is laid out, who writes it and who reads it is open to design.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes).

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Unified and System Memory (page-locked host memory).

(card: `24-host-memory-as-common-ground.md`)

### Memory and signals shared between processes

**What it is.** GPU memory allocated in one process can be opened in another, which then reads and writes the same bytes without a copy. Events can be shared the same way, so one process can wait for work another process started. Memory pools give allocation and release that are ordered with the work on a stream, and a pool can be created so that its memory is exportable.

**What it makes possible.** Work on one GPU can be split across processes without paying for copies between them.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes).

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Interprocess Communication; Stream-Ordered Memory Allocator.

(card: `11-memory-and-signals-shared-between-processes.md`)

## This machine: software

### Compiling a whole sequence of operations as one

**What it is.** The framework can trace a model's sequence of operations and hand it to a compiler as one unit, which may fuse operations, drop intermediate results and generate code specialized to the shapes seen. Separately, a sequence of GPU operations can be captured once and replayed as a graph.

**What it makes possible.** Boundaries between operations are a choice of the code as written, not of the computation.

**How this was checked.** Part of the installed framework (torch 2.14); not exercised here.

**Where to read more.** PyTorch documentation: `torch.compile`, `torch.fx`, CUDA graphs.

(card: `04-compiling-a-whole-sequence-of-operations-as-one.md`)

### Ready-made math libraries

**What it is.** NVIDIA's tuned libraries are installed: dense linear algebra (including a lightweight matrix-multiply interface with many formats, and batched forms), sparse linear algebra, matrix multiply with one operand pruned in the fixed pattern the matrix hardware supports, solvers, Fourier transforms, random numbers, the standard deep-learning operations, image and JPEG routines, and reading files directly into GPU memory.

**What it makes possible.** Some of these implement an operation in a way nobody would write by hand, and some expose hardware modes that are otherwise awkward to reach.

**How this was checked.** The packages are installed; which code paths each takes on this chip was not checked.

**Where to read more.** cuBLAS, cuSPARSE, cuSPARSELt, cuSOLVER, cuFFT, cuRAND, cuDNN, NPP, nvJPEG and cuFile documentation.

(card: `05-ready-made-math-libraries.md`)

### How Python runs

**What it is.** The Python here is 3.12. One thread at a time executes Python code within an interpreter; the others wait for a lock. Native code called from Python can release the lock while it works, and most heavy library calls do. Separate processes each have their own lock, and so can separate interpreters within one process (in this version they can only be created from native code). Starting a thread is cheap and it shares all memory; a process is isolated and shares only what is arranged.

**What it makes possible.** Whether two pieces of host work truly overlap depends on where the lock is held, not on how many threads exist.

**How this was checked.** Version read from the environment. Whether a given library call releases the lock has to be checked case by case.

**Where to read more.** Python documentation: the global interpreter lock, `threading`, `multiprocessing`.

(card: `09-how-python-runs.md`)

### Parallel building blocks

**What it is.** A library of general parallel algorithms for the GPU is installed, in C++ and with a Python interface: sort, scan (running totals), reduce, select the items that meet a condition, partition, remove duplicates, histogram, run-length encode, search, merge.

**What it makes possible.** Problems that look inherently sequential, such as compacting a list or grouping equal items, have well-known parallel forms that are already written.

**How this was checked.** The package is installed (CCCL 13.3); not exercised here.

**Where to read more.** CCCL documentation (Thrust, CUB, `cuda.cccl`).

(card: `06-parallel-building-blocks.md`)

### The communication library, beyond one operation

**What it is.** The library that moves data between GPUs (NCCL 2.30) offers more than the sum-across-GPUs operation: broadcast, reduce to one GPU, gather from all, reduce-then-scatter, and plain send and receive between two GPUs. Several operations can be grouped and issued together. Buffers can be registered ahead of time. It has a Python interface. Its newest feature, communication started from inside a kernel, needs direct GPU-to-GPU access and so does not apply on this machine.

**What it makes possible.** The exchange pattern between the GPUs is a choice from a larger set than the one in use, and the library is not the only way to move data.

**How this was checked.** The package and its Python interface are installed. Which transport it selects on this machine was not checked here.

**Where to read more.** NCCL user guide and release notes.

(card: `07-the-communication-library-beyond-one-operation.md`)
