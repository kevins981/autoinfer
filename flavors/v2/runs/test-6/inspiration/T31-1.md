# Inspiration cards

These cards are offered for inspiration only. Nobody chose them for your team, and they are not a
direction or an assignment.

- **Look at the system yourself first:** read the task, profile, find where the time goes. Then
  come back to the cards. Using none of them is fine.
- **The principle cards are general.** They say nothing about this system; applying them is your
  work.
- **The hardware and software cards are high-level pointers, not a reference.** They may be
  incomplete, out of date or wrong for your case, and each says how it was checked. Do your own
  research and verify any detail before you rely on it.

## General principles

### Timing

**The principle.** The same work costs different amounts depending on when it is done. At some moments it delays everything that waits on it; at others, capacity is free and nothing is waiting. Every piece of work has a window: it cannot start before its inputs are known, and it must finish before its output is needed. Anywhere inside the window is correct.

**The waste.** Work done at an expensive moment in its window, when a cheaper moment exists.

**General directions.**
- **Do it early:** as soon as the inputs are known, or in a quiet period.
- **Do it late:** after the urgent work, or when more is known; sometimes it turns out not to be needed.
- **Take it off the path others wait on:** the work still happens, but nothing waits for it.
- **Gather it:** hold work until a moment when it is cheap to do together.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A chef does the chopping before service starts. A bank settles the day's payments overnight. A shop restocks shelves after closing.

(card: `07-timing.md`)

### Mismatch with the machine

**The principle.** A machine is not equally good at everything. It has operations and patterns it performs very fast, and others it performs slowly; the difference can be large. The same result can usually be reached through work of different shapes. Speed depends on the fit between the shape of the work and the strengths of the machine, as much as on the amount of work.

**The waste.** Work in a shape the machine does slowly, when an equivalent shape would use what it does fast.

**General directions.**
- **Learn the machine:** what is it fast at, and which of its capabilities are rarely used?
- **Reshape the work:** sizes, order, arrangement, kind of operation, so that it fits.
- **Move work to the part that suits it:** different parts of a machine have different strengths.
- **Ask what is unused:** a capability nobody uses may fit a problem nobody matched it to.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Boxes sized to the truck bed fill it with no gaps. A car is efficient only in the right gear. Multiplying by two is a shift for a binary machine.

(card: `17-mismatch-with-the-machine.md`)

### Trade-offs

**The principle.** Improving one thing often worsens another. The usual response is to pick a point between the two. But the two needs seldom apply at the same time, in the same place, to the same things. If they can be separated, each can be met in full.

(This card is not a kind of waste. It is a way out when two goals seem to conflict.)

**The waste.** Settling for a compromise when the two needs could have been separated.

**General directions.**
- **Separate by time:** one way now, the other way later.
- **Separate by place:** one part built one way, another part the other way.
- **Separate by condition:** one way for some cases, the other way for the rest.
- **Separate by scale:** the whole behaves one way, its parts another.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Bifocal glasses give near and far vision in different parts of the lens. A road reverses a lane for the morning and the evening rush. An aircraft wing changes shape between landing and cruising.

(card: `21-trade-offs.md`)

### Predictability

**The principle.** Waiting for a result is necessary only if the result can't be predicted. If a guess is usually right, and checking a guess costs less than waiting for the answer, work can go ahead on the guess and be corrected in the rare cases it was wrong. The gain is roughly (chance of being right x time saved) - (chance of being wrong x work wasted). It needs three things: a cheap guess, a cheap check, and a way to undo.

**The waste.** Time spent waiting for an answer that was largely known in advance.

**General directions.**
- **Guess and verify:** proceed on the likely answer; check it when the real one arrives.
- **Prepare ahead:** get ready what is likely to be needed next.
- **Follow several possibilities:** and keep the one that turns out right.
- **Improve the odds or lower the price:** a better guess, a cheaper check, or a cheaper undo.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A processor guesses which way a branch will go and runs ahead. A waiter starts a regular's usual order when they walk in. A disk reads the next blocks before they are asked for.

(card: `08-predictability.md`)

### Excess guarantees

**The principle.** Every part of a system makes promises to whatever uses it: about order, exactness, freshness, completeness, timing. Each promise costs work to keep. Promises are usually set strong, because strong is safe and the users' real needs weren't known. Often what the users actually rely on is weaker.

The promises a system makes to the outside world are set by its requirements. The promises its parts make to each other are choices.

**The waste.** Work spent keeping a promise nobody relies on.

**General directions.**
- **Ask who relies on it:** for each promise, find what would break without it.
- **Weaken what nobody relies on:** while keeping everything the outside depends on.
- **Keep it only where it matters:** at the points or moments where it is relied on.
- **Keep it more cheaply:** the same promise, delivered by a different mechanism.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Not every letter needs registered mail. A bank need not post each payment the instant it is made. A list need not be fully sorted to find its largest entry.

(card: `18-excess-guarantees.md`)

## This machine: hardware

### Four one-way lanes

**What it is.** Each PCIe link carries data in both directions at once at full rate, and each GPU has its own link and two copy engines. So there are four independent one-way paths: host to GPU and GPU to host, for each of the two GPUs.

**What it makes possible.** An exchange between the GPUs does not have to be a sequence of turns. Both GPUs can send and receive at the same moment.

**How this was checked.** Follows from the link type and the device's reported copy engines; simultaneous transfers were not measured here.

**Where to read more.** PCI Express specification summaries; CUDA Programming Guide: Asynchronous Execution.

(card: `22-four-one-way-lanes.md`)

### A very large second-level cache

**What it is.** All 128 SMs of a GPU share one second-level cache of 72 MiB. That is large next to many of the arrays a program reads repeatedly.

**What it makes possible.** Data that fits in the cache and is read again soon does not have to come from main memory again. Whether it is still there depends on what else passed through the cache in between, which the order of work decides.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes).

**Where to read more.** Ada Tuning Guide; CUDA Programming Guide: L2 Cache Control.

(card: `06-a-very-large-second-level-cache.md`)

### Graphics hardware a compute program can reach

**What it is.** The chip carries hardware built for graphics that compute code can also use. Texture units fetch from a 1-, 2- or 3-dimensional array and blend the neighbouring entries in hardware in a single fetch (the blend weights have limited precision), with built-in handling of out-of-range coordinates; arrays can be up to 131,072 wide in one dimension. The ray-tracing units search a hierarchy of bounding boxes in hardware; they are reached through a separate library.

**What it makes possible.** A table lookup with interpolation, or a search over spatial data, can be done by dedicated hardware that is otherwise idle in a compute program.

**How this was checked.** Texture limits queried from the device. The ray-tracing path was not checked on this machine.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: texture and surface memory; the OptiX documentation.

(card: `18-graphics-hardware-a-compute-program-can-reach.md`)

### Control over the GPU's address space

**What it is.** A program can manage GPU memory the way an operating system manages virtual memory: reserve a range of addresses without backing it, create physical memory separately, and map, unmap or remap physical memory into the range. Physical memory can be exported to another process as a file descriptor. Sparse arrays, whose backing is supplied later piece by piece, are supported.

**What it makes possible.** A buffer can grow without being copied, memory can change hands without moving, and address layout can be chosen for convenience rather than dictated by the order of allocations.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes).

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Virtual Memory Management.

(card: `09-control-over-the-gpu-s-address-space.md`)

### Two halves of the host

**What it is.** The host is two halves (NUMA nodes), each a processor with its own memory. A core reaches the other half's memory more slowly than its own. Both GPUs attach to the first half (cores 0-11 and 24-35). A thread on the second half, or a buffer placed in the second half's memory, is one step further from both GPUs.

**What it makes possible.** Where a thread runs and where a buffer lives changes the cost of the same operation.

**How this was checked.** Read from `lscpu`, `nvidia-smi topo -m` and the devices' reported NUMA node.

**Where to read more.** `lscpu`; `/sys/devices/system/node/`.

(card: `27-two-halves-of-the-host.md`)

## This machine: software

### Compiling while the program runs

**What it is.** GPU code does not have to be compiled before the program starts. A program can generate source, compile it in memory (NVRTC), link it with other compiled pieces while still optimizing across them (nvJitLink), and load the result, all while running. The Python kernel languages here work this way already.

**What it makes possible.** At run time the program knows things that were unknown beforehand: sizes, shapes, constants, which case actually occurs. Code compiled then can have them built in.

**How this was checked.** The libraries are installed (NVRTC 13.0, nvJitLink 13.4); not exercised here.

**Where to read more.** NVRTC and nvJitLink documentation; `cuda.core` documentation.

(card: `02-compiling-while-the-program-runs.md`)

### How Python runs

**What it is.** The Python here is 3.12. One thread at a time executes Python code within an interpreter; the others wait for a lock. Native code called from Python can release the lock while it works, and most heavy library calls do. Separate processes each have their own lock, and so can separate interpreters within one process (in this version they can only be created from native code). Starting a thread is cheap and it shares all memory; a process is isolated and shares only what is arranged.

**What it makes possible.** Whether two pieces of host work truly overlap depends on where the lock is held, not on how many threads exist.

**How this was checked.** Version read from the environment. Whether a given library call releases the lock has to be checked case by case.

**Where to read more.** Python documentation: the global interpreter lock, `threading`, `multiprocessing`.

(card: `09-how-python-runs.md`)

### Ready-made math libraries

**What it is.** NVIDIA's tuned libraries are installed: dense linear algebra (including a lightweight matrix-multiply interface with many formats, and batched forms), sparse linear algebra, matrix multiply with one operand pruned in the fixed pattern the matrix hardware supports, solvers, Fourier transforms, random numbers, the standard deep-learning operations, image and JPEG routines, and reading files directly into GPU memory.

**What it makes possible.** Some of these implement an operation in a way nobody would write by hand, and some expose hardware modes that are otherwise awkward to reach.

**How this was checked.** The packages are installed; which code paths each takes on this chip was not checked.

**Where to read more.** cuBLAS, cuSPARSE, cuSPARSELt, cuSOLVER, cuFFT, cuRAND, cuDNN, NPP, nvJPEG and cuFile documentation.

(card: `05-ready-made-math-libraries.md`)

### Several levels of language for GPU code

**What it is.** GPU code can be written at different levels, and several are installed here. At thread level: CUDA C++ (compiler 13.0) and Numba (Python compiled to the same thing). At block level: Triton, where one program describes a whole thread block and the compiler arranges the threads. At tile level: NVIDIA's CuTe DSL (the building blocks of its matrix-kernel library, usable from Python) and cuTile (kernels written as operations on whole tiles).

**What it makes possible.** The level sets what the author controls and what the compiler decides. The same kernel can be cheap to write at one level and only reach full speed at another, and pieces written at different levels can live in one program.

**How this was checked.** All import in this environment. cuTile's compiler was not run on this chip.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Writing SIMT Kernels, Writing Tile Kernels; the Triton, Numba, CuTe DSL and cuTile documentation.

(card: `01-several-levels-of-language-for-gpu-code.md`)

### The framework underneath

**What it is.** The framework keeps its own pool of GPU memory and hands out pieces without asking the driver each time; the pool's behaviour can be tuned and inspected. New operations can be written in C++ or CUDA and loaded as extensions, built ahead of time or when the program starts. The framework exposes streams, events, pinned host memory and non-blocking copies. Each operation passes through a dispatch layer on the host before any GPU work is issued.

**What it makes possible.** The framework's defaults are one arrangement among several it supports, and anything it lacks can be added beside it.

**How this was checked.** From the installed framework (torch 2.14); not exercised here.

**Where to read more.** PyTorch documentation: CUDA semantics, memory management, C++ extensions.

(card: `10-the-framework-underneath.md`)
