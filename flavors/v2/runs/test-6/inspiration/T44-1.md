# Inspiration cards

These cards are offered for inspiration only. Nobody chose them for your team, and they are not a
direction or an assignment.

- **Look at the system yourself first:** read the task, profile, find where the time goes. Then
  come back to the cards. Using none of them is fine.
- **The principle cards are general.** They say nothing about this system; applying them is your
  work.
- **The hardware and software cards are high-level pointers, not a reference.** They may be
  incomplete, out of date or wrong for your case, and each says how it was checked. Do your own
  research and verify any detail before you rely on it.

## General principles

### Per-item overhead

**The principle.** The cost of a piece of work often has two parts: one that grows with its size, and a fixed part paid every time regardless of size (setting up, starting, switching, handing over). When items are small, the fixed part dominates. The fixed part belongs to the act of starting, not to the work itself, so several items can share one.

**The waste.** Fixed costs paid more often than necessary.

**General directions.**
- **Group items:** pay the fixed part once for many.
- **Join steps:** let an item pass through several steps without stopping and restarting between them.
- **Stay set up:** keep the preparation between items instead of tearing it down and rebuilding it.
- **Shrink the fixed part:** if it can't be shared, make it cheaper.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A bus carries fifty people with one driver. Laundry is done by the load. Goods cross the ocean in containers, not parcel by parcel.

(card: `10-per-item-overhead.md`)

### Trade-offs

**The principle.** Improving one thing often worsens another. The usual response is to pick a point between the two. But the two needs seldom apply at the same time, in the same place, to the same things. If they can be separated, each can be met in full.

(This card is not a kind of waste. It is a way out when two goals seem to conflict.)

**The waste.** Settling for a compromise when the two needs could have been separated.

**General directions.**
- **Separate by time:** one way now, the other way later.
- **Separate by place:** one part built one way, another part the other way.
- **Separate by condition:** one way for some cases, the other way for the rest.
- **Separate by scale:** the whole behaves one way, its parts another.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Bifocal glasses give near and far vision in different parts of the lens. A road reverses a lane for the morning and the evening rush. An aircraft wing changes shape between landing and cruising.

(card: `21-trade-offs.md`)

### Timing

**The principle.** The same work costs different amounts depending on when it is done. At some moments it delays everything that waits on it; at others, capacity is free and nothing is waiting. Every piece of work has a window: it cannot start before its inputs are known, and it must finish before its output is needed. Anywhere inside the window is correct.

**The waste.** Work done at an expensive moment in its window, when a cheaper moment exists.

**General directions.**
- **Do it early:** as soon as the inputs are known, or in a quiet period.
- **Do it late:** after the urgent work, or when more is known; sometimes it turns out not to be needed.
- **Take it off the path others wait on:** the work still happens, but nothing waits for it.
- **Gather it:** hold work until a moment when it is cheap to do together.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A chef does the chopping before service starts. A bank settles the day's payments overnight. A shop restocks shelves after closing.

(card: `07-timing.md`)

### Unused structure

**The principle.** The cost of work depends on how much the method knows about regularity in its inputs. Data or mathematics with regularity (many zeros, symmetry, sorted order, repeated patterns, few independent directions, operations that may be regrouped) contains less than its size suggests. A method that knows the regularity can do proportionally less.

**The waste.** Work in proportion to the raw size of the problem, when its real content is smaller.

**General directions.**
- **Look for regularity:** measure it; don't assume it is absent.
- **Skip what contributes nothing:** parts known to be zero, unchanged or irrelevant.
- **Do one of a kind:** when parts are symmetric or repeated, do one and reuse it.
- **Use the laws of the mathematics:** regroup, factor or reorder to reach a cheaper equivalent form.
- **Choose a method whose cost follows the content:** not the size.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Sorted order lets you find a name in a phone book in a few steps. Multiplying by a table that is mostly zeros needs only the non-zero entries. Gauss summed 1 to 100 by pairing the ends.

(card: `04-unused-structure.md`)

### Imbalance

**The principle.** A system has several resources: time, memory, capacity to move things, different kinds of processing. They are rarely all scarce at once. Usually one limits the system and the others have room to spare. Most work can be done in more than one way, each using a different mix of resources. Gains come only at the limiting resource; spending the others costs nothing until they run out.

**The waste.** A plentiful resource left unused while a scarce one limits the system.

**General directions.**
- **Find the limit:** which resource is the system actually waiting for?
- **Spend the plentiful to save the scarce:** choose the way of working that leans on what is spare.
- **Trade in either direction:** more memory for less work, or more work for less memory or movement.
- **Check again:** after a change the limit may have moved to another resource.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A table of answers spends space to save time; compression spends time to save space. A slow cooker spends hours to save attention. A factory with idle machines and too few workers reorganizes around the workers.

(card: `14-imbalance.md`)

## This machine: hardware

### How the host learns that the GPU is done

**What it is.** When a host thread waits for GPU work, it can spin on a core, yield its core between checks, or sleep until an interrupt arrives. These differ in how fast the thread reacts and how much processor time it burns. Besides waiting, the host can poll an event without blocking, or attach a function that runs when a stream reaches a given point. The device also reports stream operations that write a value to memory or wait until memory holds a value.

**What it makes possible.** The gap between the GPU finishing and the host acting on it is set by the waiting method, and it is paid at every hand-over.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes). The waiting modes are from the documentation.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Asynchronous Execution; driver API: context scheduling flags, stream memory operations.

(card: `16-how-the-host-learns-that-the-gpu-is-done.md`)

### Fetching without waiting, inside a kernel

**What it is.** A kernel can start a copy from main memory into its scratch memory and continue working while the copy is in flight. The data does not pass through registers. Barrier objects tell the kernel when a batch of copies has landed, and several batches can be in flight at once (a pipeline).

**What it makes possible.** Fetching the next piece of data can overlap with computing on the current piece, inside a single kernel, instead of the two alternating.

**How this was checked.** Each instruction was assembled for this chip (`ptxas -arch=sm_89`); not run. The bulk form of this copy is not available on this chip.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Asynchronous Data Copies; Pipelines; Asynchronous Barriers.

(card: `08-fetching-without-waiting-inside-a-kernel.md`)

### Links that slow down when idle

**What it is.** A PCIe link can drop to a lower speed or a low-power state when idle and return to full speed when traffic resumes. On this machine an idle GPU's link was seen reporting generation 1 instead of 4. Coming back to full speed takes time.

**What it makes possible.** The first transfer after a quiet period may not see the same link as a transfer in a steady stream.

**How this was checked.** The idle link speed was read from `nvidia-smi`. The time to return to full speed was not measured.

**Where to read more.** `nvidia-smi -q` (GPU link info); PCI Express power management summaries.

(card: `25-links-that-slow-down-when-idle.md`)

### Memory that lives on both sides

**What it is.** Unified (managed) memory is one address range valid on the host and on the GPU. Pages move to whichever side touches them, and both sides can hold pages at the same time. A program can give hints: where a range should preferably live, that it is mostly read, or that it should be moved now ahead of use. A range may be larger than the GPU's free memory; pages are evicted to the host as needed. Ordinary pageable host memory cannot be read by the GPU directly on this system.

**What it makes possible.** Data need not have one fixed home, and the total can exceed what the GPU holds, at the price of page moves when the wrong side touches a page.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes).

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Unified Memory.

(card: `10-memory-that-lives-on-both-sides.md`)

### Several processes on one GPU

**What it is.** By default, processes that use the same GPU take turns: the GPU switches between them in time slices, and only one runs at any instant. The multi-process service (MPS) funnels several processes into one shared context so their kernels can run at the same time, and can cap each process's share of the GPU. An ordinary user can start the service for their own processes; its control program is installed. It is not running now, and other people use this machine.

**What it makes possible.** Whether work split across processes overlaps or alternates on the GPU is a choice, not a fixed property.

**How this was checked.** The device reports the default sharing mode and that MPS is off. Starting MPS was not tried.

**Where to read more.** NVIDIA Multi-Process Service documentation.

(card: `14-several-processes-on-one-gpu.md`)

## This machine: software

### Compiling a whole sequence of operations as one

**What it is.** The framework can trace a model's sequence of operations and hand it to a compiler as one unit, which may fuse operations, drop intermediate results and generate code specialized to the shapes seen. Separately, a sequence of GPU operations can be captured once and replayed as a graph.

**What it makes possible.** Boundaries between operations are a choice of the code as written, not of the computation.

**How this was checked.** Part of the installed framework (torch 2.14); not exercised here.

**Where to read more.** PyTorch documentation: `torch.compile`, `torch.fx`, CUDA graphs.

(card: `04-compiling-a-whole-sequence-of-operations-as-one.md`)

### Ready-made math libraries

**What it is.** NVIDIA's tuned libraries are installed: dense linear algebra (including a lightweight matrix-multiply interface with many formats, and batched forms), sparse linear algebra, matrix multiply with one operand pruned in the fixed pattern the matrix hardware supports, solvers, Fourier transforms, random numbers, the standard deep-learning operations, image and JPEG routines, and reading files directly into GPU memory.

**What it makes possible.** Some of these implement an operation in a way nobody would write by hand, and some expose hardware modes that are otherwise awkward to reach.

**How this was checked.** The packages are installed; which code paths each takes on this chip was not checked.

**Where to read more.** cuBLAS, cuSPARSE, cuSPARSELt, cuSOLVER, cuFFT, cuRAND, cuDNN, NPP, nvJPEG and cuFile documentation.

(card: `05-ready-made-math-libraries.md`)

### Compiling while the program runs

**What it is.** GPU code does not have to be compiled before the program starts. A program can generate source, compile it in memory (NVRTC), link it with other compiled pieces while still optimizing across them (nvJitLink), and load the result, all while running. The Python kernel languages here work this way already.

**What it makes possible.** At run time the program knows things that were unknown beforehand: sizes, shapes, constants, which case actually occurs. Code compiled then can have them built in.

**How this was checked.** The libraries are installed (NVRTC 13.0, nvJitLink 13.4); not exercised here.

**Where to read more.** NVRTC and nvJitLink documentation; `cuda.core` documentation.

(card: `02-compiling-while-the-program-runs.md`)

### Several levels of language for GPU code

**What it is.** GPU code can be written at different levels, and several are installed here. At thread level: CUDA C++ (compiler 13.0) and Numba (Python compiled to the same thing). At block level: Triton, where one program describes a whole thread block and the compiler arranges the threads. At tile level: NVIDIA's CuTe DSL (the building blocks of its matrix-kernel library, usable from Python) and cuTile (kernels written as operations on whole tiles).

**What it makes possible.** The level sets what the author controls and what the compiler decides. The same kernel can be cheap to write at one level and only reach full speed at another, and pieces written at different levels can live in one program.

**How this was checked.** All import in this environment. cuTile's compiler was not run on this chip.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Writing SIMT Kernels, Writing Tile Kernels; the Triton, Numba, CuTe DSL and cuTile documentation.

(card: `01-several-levels-of-language-for-gpu-code.md`)

### The communication library, beyond one operation

**What it is.** The library that moves data between GPUs (NCCL 2.30) offers more than the sum-across-GPUs operation: broadcast, reduce to one GPU, gather from all, reduce-then-scatter, and plain send and receive between two GPUs. Several operations can be grouped and issued together. Buffers can be registered ahead of time. It has a Python interface. Its newest feature, communication started from inside a kernel, needs direct GPU-to-GPU access and so does not apply on this machine.

**What it makes possible.** The exchange pattern between the GPUs is a choice from a larger set than the one in use, and the library is not the only way to move data.

**How this was checked.** The package and its Python interface are installed. Which transport it selects on this machine was not checked here.

**Where to read more.** NCCL user guide and release notes.

(card: `07-the-communication-library-beyond-one-operation.md`)
