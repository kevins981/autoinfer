# Inspiration cards

These cards are offered for inspiration only. Nobody chose them for your team, and they are not a
direction or an assignment.

- **Look at the system yourself first:** read the task, profile, find where the time goes. Then
  come back to the cards. Using none of them is fine.
- **The principle cards are general.** They say nothing about this system; applying them is your
  work.
- **The hardware and software cards are high-level pointers, not a reference.** They may be
  incomplete, out of date or wrong for your case, and each says how it was checked. Do your own
  research and verify any detail before you rely on it.

## General principles

### Overqualified work

**The principle.** Expensive work is often applied to many candidates, of which only a few matter to the final answer. If a cheap test can tell which candidates cannot matter, the expensive work is needed only for the rest. The final answer still comes from the expensive, exact work, so it stays exact. The cheap test only has to avoid ruling out a candidate that matters.

**The waste.** Exact work spent on candidates that a cheaper test would have eliminated.

**General directions.**
- **Cheap pass first:** then exact work on the survivors only.
- **Use bounds:** a cheap bound can prove a candidate cannot win without evaluating it.
- **Stages of rising cost:** each stage removes candidates before the next, dearer one.
- **Order the tests:** cheapest and most selective first.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Security screens everyone quickly and searches a few by hand. An index rules out most rows before any is read. A chess program drops moves that a bound shows cannot beat the best found so far.

(card: `05-overqualified-work.md`)

### Rework

**The principle.** Some work is done and then thrown away: an attempt fails, a guess is wrong, a result arrives too late or is made invalid, something is retried from the start. Its cost was paid and it contributed nothing. This is often accepted as rare, and rarely measured.

**The waste.** All work whose result is discarded.

**General directions.**
- **Measure it:** how much work is thrown away, and why?
- **Detect the failure earlier:** before more work is built on top of it.
- **Keep what is still valid:** discard only the part that was wrong.
- **Fail less often:** remove the cause of the discarded attempts.
- **Make a failed attempt cheaper:** so that being wrong costs little.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A carpenter measures twice and cuts once. A long calculation saves its progress so a failure doesn't restart it. A factory inspects at each station, not only at the end of the line.

(card: `19-rework.md`)

### Coordination

**The principle.** When parts work at the same time on related things, they must agree: wait for each other, take turns on what they share, exchange what they know. This work produces no output. It exists only because the work was divided. It grows with how much is shared and how often agreement is required, not with the amount of useful work.

**The waste.** Time spent agreeing, and waiting to agree.

**General directions.**
- **Share less:** divide the work so the parts need little from each other.
- **Agree less often:** exchange at longer intervals, or only when something changed.
- **Agree on less:** only what must be common; the rest can stay local.
- **Proceed and reconcile:** let parts continue without waiting, and settle differences afterwards.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A team's meetings grow faster than the team. A roundabout lets cars flow without waiting for a signal. Each cook in a kitchen has their own station.

(card: `12-coordination.md`)

### Imbalance

**The principle.** A system has several resources: time, memory, capacity to move things, different kinds of processing. They are rarely all scarce at once. Usually one limits the system and the others have room to spare. Most work can be done in more than one way, each using a different mix of resources. Gains come only at the limiting resource; spending the others costs nothing until they run out.

**The waste.** A plentiful resource left unused while a scarce one limits the system.

**General directions.**
- **Find the limit:** which resource is the system actually waiting for?
- **Spend the plentiful to save the scarce:** choose the way of working that leans on what is spare.
- **Trade in either direction:** more memory for less work, or more work for less memory or movement.
- **Check again:** after a change the limit may have moved to another resource.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A table of answers spends space to save time; compression spends time to save space. A slow cooker spends hours to save attention. A factory with idle machines and too few workers reorganizes around the workers.

(card: `14-imbalance.md`)

### Trade-offs

**The principle.** Improving one thing often worsens another. The usual response is to pick a point between the two. But the two needs seldom apply at the same time, in the same place, to the same things. If they can be separated, each can be met in full.

(This card is not a kind of waste. It is a way out when two goals seem to conflict.)

**The waste.** Settling for a compromise when the two needs could have been separated.

**General directions.**
- **Separate by time:** one way now, the other way later.
- **Separate by place:** one part built one way, another part the other way.
- **Separate by condition:** one way for some cases, the other way for the rest.
- **Separate by scale:** the whole behaves one way, its parts another.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Bifocal glasses give near and far vision in different parts of the lens. A road reverses a lane for the morning and the evening rush. An aircraft wing changes shape between landing and cruising.

(card: `21-trade-offs.md`)

## This machine: hardware

### Control over the GPU's address space

**What it is.** A program can manage GPU memory the way an operating system manages virtual memory: reserve a range of addresses without backing it, create physical memory separately, and map, unmap or remap physical memory into the range. Physical memory can be exported to another process as a file descriptor. Sparse arrays, whose backing is supplied later piece by piece, are supported.

**What it makes possible.** A buffer can grow without being copied, memory can change hands without moving, and address layout can be chosen for convenience rather than dictated by the order of allocations.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes).

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Virtual Memory Management.

(card: `09-control-over-the-gpu-s-address-space.md`)

### Graphics hardware a compute program can reach

**What it is.** The chip carries hardware built for graphics that compute code can also use. Texture units fetch from a 1-, 2- or 3-dimensional array and blend the neighbouring entries in hardware in a single fetch (the blend weights have limited precision), with built-in handling of out-of-range coordinates; arrays can be up to 131,072 wide in one dimension. The ray-tracing units search a hierarchy of bounding boxes in hardware; they are reached through a separate library.

**What it makes possible.** A table lookup with interpolation, or a search over spatial data, can be done by dedicated hardware that is otherwise idle in a compute program.

**How this was checked.** Texture limits queried from the device. The ray-tracing path was not checked on this machine.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: texture and surface memory; the OptiX documentation.

(card: `18-graphics-hardware-a-compute-program-can-reach.md`)

### Host memory

**What it is.** 125 GB of DDR5 memory across the two halves, shared with other users. The processors support 8 memory channels each at up to 4,000 MT/s; how many are populated was not checked. Host memory is slower to read than GPU memory (how much slower was not measured), but there is five times as much of it as one GPU has. A memory-backed file system (`/dev/shm`, 63 GB) lets processes share memory by name.

**What it makes possible.** The host has room to hold things the GPUs cannot, at the cost of the link to reach them.

**How this was checked.** Sizes read from `free` and `df`. Memory speed was not measured.

**Where to read more.** `lscpu`, `dmidecode` output where readable without root.

(card: `28-host-memory.md`)

### Memory that lives on both sides

**What it is.** Unified (managed) memory is one address range valid on the host and on the GPU. Pages move to whichever side touches them, and both sides can hold pages at the same time. A program can give hints: where a range should preferably live, that it is mostly read, or that it should be moved now ahead of use. A range may be larger than the GPU's free memory; pages are evicted to the host as needed. Ordinary pageable host memory cannot be read by the GPU directly on this system.

**What it makes possible.** Data need not have one fixed home, and the total can exceed what the GPU holds, at the price of page moves when the wrong side touches a page.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes).

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Unified Memory.

(card: `10-memory-that-lives-on-both-sides.md`)

### Four one-way lanes

**What it is.** Each PCIe link carries data in both directions at once at full rate, and each GPU has its own link and two copy engines. So there are four independent one-way paths: host to GPU and GPU to host, for each of the two GPUs.

**What it makes possible.** An exchange between the GPUs does not have to be a sequence of turns. Both GPUs can send and receive at the same moment.

**How this was checked.** Follows from the link type and the device's reported copy engines; simultaneous transfers were not measured here.

**Where to read more.** PCI Express specification summaries; CUDA Programming Guide: Asynchronous Execution.

(card: `22-four-one-way-lanes.md`)

## This machine: software

### How Python runs

**What it is.** The Python here is 3.12. One thread at a time executes Python code within an interpreter; the others wait for a lock. Native code called from Python can release the lock while it works, and most heavy library calls do. Separate processes each have their own lock, and so can separate interpreters within one process (in this version they can only be created from native code). Starting a thread is cheap and it shares all memory; a process is isolated and shares only what is arranged.

**What it makes possible.** Whether two pieces of host work truly overlap depends on where the lock is held, not on how many threads exist.

**How this was checked.** Version read from the environment. Whether a given library call releases the lock has to be checked case by case.

**Where to read more.** Python documentation: the global interpreter lock, `threading`, `multiprocessing`.

(card: `09-how-python-runs.md`)

### Several levels of language for GPU code

**What it is.** GPU code can be written at different levels, and several are installed here. At thread level: CUDA C++ (compiler 13.0) and Numba (Python compiled to the same thing). At block level: Triton, where one program describes a whole thread block and the compiler arranges the threads. At tile level: NVIDIA's CuTe DSL (the building blocks of its matrix-kernel library, usable from Python) and cuTile (kernels written as operations on whole tiles).

**What it makes possible.** The level sets what the author controls and what the compiler decides. The same kernel can be cheap to write at one level and only reach full speed at another, and pieces written at different levels can live in one program.

**How this was checked.** All import in this environment. cuTile's compiler was not run on this chip.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Writing SIMT Kernels, Writing Tile Kernels; the Triton, Numba, CuTe DSL and cuTile documentation.

(card: `01-several-levels-of-language-for-gpu-code.md`)

### The operating system: what a user can control

**What it is.** Without administrator rights a user can still: choose which cores a thread or process may run on; choose that a thread's memory is allocated on the half of the host where it first touches it; request large pages for a memory range (the system grants them on request, not by default); lock up to about 15.7 GB of memory so it is never paged out; lower a process's priority; create shared memory. A tool to set core affinity is installed; the usual tool for memory placement is not, though the underlying system calls are available.

**What it makes possible.** Where threads run and where memory sits is left to the system's defaults unless the program says otherwise.

**How this was checked.** Read from `ulimit`, `/sys/kernel/mm/transparent_hugepage/` and the installed tools.

**Where to read more.** `man` pages: `sched_setaffinity`, `mbind`, `madvise`, `mlock`, `taskset`.

(card: `11-the-operating-system-what-a-user-can-control.md`)

### The operating system: what a user cannot control

**What it is.** Some conditions are fixed for an ordinary user. The processor frequency policy raises a core's speed only after load appears on it. The system may move a process's memory pages between the two halves of the host on its own. No cores are reserved: any thread can be interrupted or moved to make room for another, and real-time priority is not available. Other people use the machine. The driver version (580) caps the CUDA version at 13.0. The kernel is Linux 5.15.

**What it makes possible.** These are the conditions any design has to work under. A design that depends on a quiet, pinned, fully warmed-up host will not always get one.

**How this was checked.** Read from `/sys` and `/proc` on this machine.

**Where to read more.** Linux kernel documentation: CPU frequency scaling, NUMA balancing, scheduler.

(card: `12-the-operating-system-what-a-user-cannot-control.md`)

### Ready-made math libraries

**What it is.** NVIDIA's tuned libraries are installed: dense linear algebra (including a lightweight matrix-multiply interface with many formats, and batched forms), sparse linear algebra, matrix multiply with one operand pruned in the fixed pattern the matrix hardware supports, solvers, Fourier transforms, random numbers, the standard deep-learning operations, image and JPEG routines, and reading files directly into GPU memory.

**What it makes possible.** Some of these implement an operation in a way nobody would write by hand, and some expose hardware modes that are otherwise awkward to reach.

**How this was checked.** The packages are installed; which code paths each takes on this chip was not checked.

**Where to read more.** cuBLAS, cuSPARSE, cuSPARSELt, cuSOLVER, cuFFT, cuRAND, cuDNN, NPP, nvJPEG and cuFile documentation.

(card: `05-ready-made-math-libraries.md`)
