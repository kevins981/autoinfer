# Inspiration cards

These cards are offered for inspiration only. Nobody chose them for your team, and they are not a
direction or an assignment.

- **Look at the system yourself first:** read the task, profile, find where the time goes. Then
  come back to the cards. Using none of them is fine.
- **The principle cards are general.** They say nothing about this system; applying them is your
  work.
- **The hardware and software cards are high-level pointers, not a reference.** They may be
  incomplete, out of date or wrong for your case, and each says how it was checked. Do your own
  research and verify any detail before you rely on it.

## General principles

### Repetition

**The principle.** Any piece of work is a function: it takes inputs and produces an output. The output contains nothing that wasn't already determined by the inputs. So work is only necessary when the inputs are new. If the inputs were seen before, the output already exists somewhere. If part of the inputs is new, only the part of the output that depends on it needs to be made.

This holds for computing a value, and equally for moving or storing one: sending something to a place that already has it is the same waste.

**The waste.** The work done, minus the work the new information required.

**General directions.**
- **Remember:** keep an output so the work isn't done again. This trades memory for work.
- **Share:** produce an output once and let everyone who needs it use the same copy.
- **Update by difference:** when the inputs change a little, compute only what the change affects.
- **Do it ahead:** if the inputs are known long before the output is needed, the work can be done once, early.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Incremental compilation rebuilds only what a change touched. Rsync sends only the parts of a file the other side lacks. A spreadsheet recomputes only the cells that depend on the edited one.

(card: `01-repetition.md`)

### Boundaries

**The principle.** Systems are divided into parts so that each can be built and understood on its own. Every boundary has two costs:
- **Crossing:** things are converted, copied, checked and handed over.
- **Blindness:** each part decides with only what it can see. It can't use what its neighbours know, so it must assume the worst.

**The waste.** The cost of crossing, plus the better decisions missed because knowledge stops at the boundary.

**General directions.**
- **Pass knowledge across:** tell the other side what you know, so it needn't assume or rediscover it.
- **Merge parts:** that work on the same thing.
- **Go around a layer:** when the service it provides isn't needed on this path.
- **Move the boundary:** to where fewer things cross it.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Two people who share a language need no interpreter. A form that passes through five departments waits at each desk. A compiler that sees across a function call can simplify both sides.

(card: `11-boundaries.md`)

### Unused structure

**The principle.** The cost of work depends on how much the method knows about regularity in its inputs. Data or mathematics with regularity (many zeros, symmetry, sorted order, repeated patterns, few independent directions, operations that may be regrouped) contains less than its size suggests. A method that knows the regularity can do proportionally less.

**The waste.** Work in proportion to the raw size of the problem, when its real content is smaller.

**General directions.**
- **Look for regularity:** measure it; don't assume it is absent.
- **Skip what contributes nothing:** parts known to be zero, unchanged or irrelevant.
- **Do one of a kind:** when parts are symmetric or repeated, do one and reuse it.
- **Use the laws of the mathematics:** regroup, factor or reorder to reach a cheaper equivalent form.
- **Choose a method whose cost follows the content:** not the size.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Sorted order lets you find a name in a phone book in a few steps. Multiplying by a table that is mostly zeros needs only the non-zero entries. Gauss summed 1 to 100 by pairing the ends.

(card: `04-unused-structure.md`)

### Per-item overhead

**The principle.** The cost of a piece of work often has two parts: one that grows with its size, and a fixed part paid every time regardless of size (setting up, starting, switching, handing over). When items are small, the fixed part dominates. The fixed part belongs to the act of starting, not to the work itself, so several items can share one.

**The waste.** Fixed costs paid more often than necessary.

**General directions.**
- **Group items:** pay the fixed part once for many.
- **Join steps:** let an item pass through several steps without stopping and restarting between them.
- **Stay set up:** keep the preparation between items instead of tearing it down and rebuilding it.
- **Shrink the fixed part:** if it can't be shared, make it cheaper.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A bus carries fifty people with one driver. Laundry is done by the load. Goods cross the ocean in containers, not parcel by parcel.

(card: `10-per-item-overhead.md`)

### Fixed choices

**The principle.** Many decisions (a size, a limit, an order, a method) are made once, in advance, from a guess about conditions. But conditions vary: between inputs, with load, over time. A fixed choice is right for one condition at most and somewhat wrong for the others. The information needed for a better choice often exists at the moment of use and costs little to observe.

**The waste.** The gap between the fixed choice and the best choice for the conditions that actually occur.

**General directions.**
- **Observe, then choose:** look at the conditions before deciding.
- **Prepare several versions:** and select among them at the moment of use.
- **Adjust by feedback:** measure the result and correct the choice continuously.
- **Decide later:** postpone the decision until more is known.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A thermostat replaces a heater that is always on. Traffic lights change their timing with the traffic. A gearbox picks a ratio for the current speed.

(card: `16-fixed-choices.md`)

## This machine: hardware

### Fetching without waiting, inside a kernel

**What it is.** A kernel can start a copy from main memory into its scratch memory and continue working while the copy is in flight. The data does not pass through registers. Barrier objects tell the kernel when a batch of copies has landed, and several batches can be in flight at once (a pipeline).

**What it makes possible.** Fetching the next piece of data can overlap with computing on the current piece, inside a single kernel, instead of the two alternating.

**How this was checked.** Each instruction was assembled for this chip (`ptxas -arch=sm_89`); not run. The bulk form of this copy is not available on this chip.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Asynchronous Data Copies; Pipelines; Asynchronous Barriers.

(card: `08-fetching-without-waiting-inside-a-kernel.md`)

### No direct path between the two GPUs

**What it is.** On this machine one GPU cannot read or write the other's memory (peer-to-peer access is reported as not supported), and there is no dedicated GPU-to-GPU link. Data going from one GPU to the other travels up one link into host memory and down the other link. Libraries that depend on direct access between GPUs do not apply here.

**What it makes possible.** Every exchange between the GPUs has two hops and passes through memory the host processors can also see. The host is on the path whether it takes part or not.

**How this was checked.** `nvidia-smi topo -p2p r` reports peer-to-peer as not supported.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Programming Systems with Multiple GPUs.

(card: `21-no-direct-path-between-the-two-gpus.md`)

### Copy engines beside the compute engine

**What it is.** Each GPU reports two copy engines, separate from the hardware that runs kernels. A copy between host and GPU can proceed while a kernel runs, and a copy in each direction can proceed at the same time.

**What it makes possible.** Moving data on or off a GPU does not have to stop computation on it, and sending does not have to wait for receiving.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes).

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Asynchronous Execution.

(card: `17-copy-engines-beside-the-compute-engine.md`)

### Main memory and how fast it can be read

**What it is.** Each GPU has 24 GB of GDDR6X memory on a 384-bit bus clocked at 10,501 MHz. At 21 Gbit/s per pin that is a theoretical peak of 1,008 GB/s. There is no error correction. The memory is not shared between the two GPUs.

**What it makes possible.** The peak rate puts a floor under any step that must read a given number of bytes: bytes divided by rate. Comparing that floor with a step's measured time shows whether the step is limited by reading memory or by something else.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes). The peak rate is arithmetic from the bus width and data rate, not a measurement.

**Where to read more.** Ada Tuning Guide; the Nsight Compute memory metrics.

(card: `05-main-memory-and-how-fast-it-can-be-read.md`)

### Control over the GPU's address space

**What it is.** A program can manage GPU memory the way an operating system manages virtual memory: reserve a range of addresses without backing it, create physical memory separately, and map, unmap or remap physical memory into the range. Physical memory can be exported to another process as a file descriptor. Sparse arrays, whose backing is supplied later piece by piece, are supported.

**What it makes possible.** A buffer can grow without being copied, memory can change hands without moving, and address layout can be chosen for convenience rather than dictated by the order of allocations.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes).

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Virtual Memory Management.

(card: `09-control-over-the-gpu-s-address-space.md`)

## This machine: software

### How Python runs

**What it is.** The Python here is 3.12. One thread at a time executes Python code within an interpreter; the others wait for a lock. Native code called from Python can release the lock while it works, and most heavy library calls do. Separate processes each have their own lock, and so can separate interpreters within one process (in this version they can only be created from native code). Starting a thread is cheap and it shares all memory; a process is isolated and shares only what is arranged.

**What it makes possible.** Whether two pieces of host work truly overlap depends on where the lock is held, not on how many threads exist.

**How this was checked.** Version read from the environment. Whether a given library call releases the lock has to be checked case by case.

**Where to read more.** Python documentation: the global interpreter lock, `threading`, `multiprocessing`.

(card: `09-how-python-runs.md`)

### Several levels of language for GPU code

**What it is.** GPU code can be written at different levels, and several are installed here. At thread level: CUDA C++ (compiler 13.0) and Numba (Python compiled to the same thing). At block level: Triton, where one program describes a whole thread block and the compiler arranges the threads. At tile level: NVIDIA's CuTe DSL (the building blocks of its matrix-kernel library, usable from Python) and cuTile (kernels written as operations on whole tiles).

**What it makes possible.** The level sets what the author controls and what the compiler decides. The same kernel can be cheap to write at one level and only reach full speed at another, and pieces written at different levels can live in one program.

**How this was checked.** All import in this environment. cuTile's compiler was not run on this chip.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Writing SIMT Kernels, Writing Tile Kernels; the Triton, Numba, CuTe DSL and cuTile documentation.

(card: `01-several-levels-of-language-for-gpu-code.md`)

### Down to the instructions

**What it is.** The compiler's intermediate language (PTX) can be read, written by hand, and placed inline inside C++ code. The assembler reports registers used and spilled per kernel. The final machine instructions can be disassembled from any compiled kernel, including ones from libraries. The profiler can attribute time to single source lines and instructions.

**What it makes possible.** What a kernel really does is visible, and what the higher-level languages do not expose can still be written.

**How this was checked.** The tools are installed (`ptxas`, `nvdisasm`, `cuobjdump`) and the assembler was used to check the hardware cards.

**Where to read more.** PTX ISA reference; CUDA binary utilities documentation.

(card: `03-down-to-the-instructions.md`)

### Ready-made math libraries

**What it is.** NVIDIA's tuned libraries are installed: dense linear algebra (including a lightweight matrix-multiply interface with many formats, and batched forms), sparse linear algebra, matrix multiply with one operand pruned in the fixed pattern the matrix hardware supports, solvers, Fourier transforms, random numbers, the standard deep-learning operations, image and JPEG routines, and reading files directly into GPU memory.

**What it makes possible.** Some of these implement an operation in a way nobody would write by hand, and some expose hardware modes that are otherwise awkward to reach.

**How this was checked.** The packages are installed; which code paths each takes on this chip was not checked.

**Where to read more.** cuBLAS, cuSPARSE, cuSPARSELt, cuSOLVER, cuFFT, cuRAND, cuDNN, NPP, nvJPEG and cuFile documentation.

(card: `05-ready-made-math-libraries.md`)

### Sharing without copying

**What it is.** Several mechanisms let two parties use the same bytes. Host processes can share memory by name (63 GB available in the memory-backed file system). GPU memory can be shared between processes. Libraries in one process can hand each other GPU arrays without a copy through a standard exchange format (DLPack) or a common array interface. Message passing between processes is available with zero-copy for large messages.

**What it makes possible.** A boundary between processes or between libraries does not have to cost a copy.

**How this was checked.** The mechanisms are present in the installed software; not exercised here.

**Where to read more.** DLPack; PyTorch `torch.multiprocessing`; CUDA Programming Guide: Interprocess Communication; ZeroMQ guide.

(card: `08-sharing-without-copying.md`)
