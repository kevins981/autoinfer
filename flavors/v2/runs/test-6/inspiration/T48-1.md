# Inspiration cards

These cards are offered for inspiration only. Nobody chose them for your team, and they are not a
direction or an assignment.

  incomplete, out of date or wrong for your case, and each says how it was checked. Do your own
  research and verify any detail before you rely on it.

## General principles

### Unused structure

**The principle.** The cost of work depends on how much the method knows about regularity in its inputs. Data or mathematics with regularity (many zeros, symmetry, sorted order, repeated patterns, few independent directions, operations that may be regrouped) contains less than its size suggests. A method that knows the regularity can do proportionally less.

**The waste.** Work in proportion to the raw size of the problem, when its real content is smaller.

**General directions.**
- **Look for regularity:** measure it; don't assume it is absent.
- **Skip what contributes nothing:** parts known to be zero, unchanged or irrelevant.
- **Do one of a kind:** when parts are symmetric or repeated, do one and reuse it.
- **Use the laws of the mathematics:** regroup, factor or reorder to reach a cheaper equivalent form.
- **Choose a method whose cost follows the content:** not the size.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Sorted order lets you find a name in a phone book in a few steps. Multiplying by a table that is mostly zeros needs only the non-zero entries. Gauss summed 1 to 100 by pairing the ends.

(card: `04-unused-structure.md`)

### Holding

**The principle.** Things made or reserved before they are needed occupy capacity while they wait: space, slots, attention. Capacity is finite, so whatever is held ahead of need displaces something else. Holding is usually chosen for safety, to be ready for the worst case. Its cost is hidden, because it shows up as a shortage somewhere else.

**The waste.** Capacity occupied by things that are not in use.

**General directions.**
- **Find what is held:** and how much of it is in use at any moment.
- **Reserve for actual need:** not for the worst case, with a way to get more when needed.
- **Release sooner:** give capacity back as soon as it is no longer used.
- **Share reserves:** among users who are unlikely to need them at the same time.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A factory takes delivery of parts as they are needed instead of filling a warehouse. An airline sells more seats than the plane has, knowing some passengers won't come. An office shares desks among people who are rarely in on the same day.

(card: `20-holding.md`)

### Representation

**The principle.** The same information can be written in many forms. Forms differ in size, and in which operations are cheap on them. The cost of storing, moving and processing follows the form, not the information. A form is usually chosen once, for convenience, and everything afterwards pays for it.

**The waste.** Extra size and extra steps caused by the form, not by the information.

**General directions.**
- **A smaller form:** no larger than the information it holds.
- **A form that makes the frequent operation cheap:** even if rare operations get dearer.
- **Arrange for use:** put next to each other what is used together.
- **Convert once:** at the edge, not repeatedly inside.
- **Several forms:** the same information kept in different forms for different uses.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Arithmetic is easy in Arabic numerals and hard in Roman ones. A phone book is sorted by name because that is how it is searched. A map and a list of turns hold the same route for different uses.

(card: `13-representation.md`)

### Rework

**The principle.** Some work is done and then thrown away: an attempt fails, a guess is wrong, a result arrives too late or is made invalid, something is retried from the start. Its cost was paid and it contributed nothing. This is often accepted as rare, and rarely measured.

**The waste.** All work whose result is discarded.

**General directions.**
- **Measure it:** how much work is thrown away, and why?
- **Detect the failure earlier:** before more work is built on top of it.
- **Keep what is still valid:** discard only the part that was wrong.
- **Fail less often:** remove the cause of the discarded attempts.
- **Make a failed attempt cheaper:** so that being wrong costs little.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A carpenter measures twice and cuts once. A long calculation saves its progress so a failure doesn't restart it. A factory inspects at each station, not only at the end of the line.

(card: `19-rework.md`)

### Per-item overhead

**The principle.** The cost of a piece of work often has two parts: one that grows with its size, and a fixed part paid every time regardless of size (setting up, starting, switching, handing over). When items are small, the fixed part dominates. The fixed part belongs to the act of starting, not to the work itself, so several items can share one.

**The waste.** Fixed costs paid more often than necessary.

**General directions.**
- **Group items:** pay the fixed part once for many.
- **Join steps:** let an item pass through several steps without stopping and restarting between them.
- **Stay set up:** keep the preparation between items instead of tearing it down and rebuilding it.
- **Shrink the fixed part:** if it can't be shared, make it cheaper.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A bus carries fifty people with one driver. Laundry is done by the load. Goods cross the ocean in containers, not parcel by parcel.

(card: `10-per-item-overhead.md`)

## This machine: hardware

### Fetching without waiting, inside a kernel

**What it is.** A kernel can start a copy from main memory into its scratch memory and continue working while the copy is in flight. The data does not pass through registers. Barrier objects tell the kernel when a batch of copies has landed, and several batches can be in flight at once (a pipeline).

**What it makes possible.** Fetching the next piece of data can overlap with computing on the current piece, inside a single kernel, instead of the two alternating.

**How this was checked.** Each instruction was assembled for this chip (`ptxas -arch=sm_89`); not run. The bulk form of this copy is not available on this chip.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Asynchronous Data Copies; Pipelines; Asynchronous Barriers.

(card: `08-fetching-without-waiting-inside-a-kernel.md`)

### The host processors

**What it is.** Two Intel Xeon Silver 4410Y processors: 12 cores each (24 cores, 48 hardware threads), 2.0 GHz base and up to 3.9 GHz. Each core has 2 MiB of second-level cache; each processor has 30 MiB of third-level cache. They have 512-bit vector instructions, including 16-bit float and bfloat16 arithmetic and 8-bit integer dot products, and a matrix extension (AMX) that multiplies tiles of bfloat16 or 8-bit integers.

**What it makes possible.** The host is slow next to a GPU but far from negligible, it is mostly idle while the GPUs work, and it sits on the path between them.

**How this was checked.** Read from `lscpu`.

**Where to read more.** Intel's processor specification page; Intel intrinsics guide.

(card: `26-the-host-processors.md`)

### Host memory

**What it is.** 125 GB of DDR5 memory across the two halves, shared with other users. The processors support 8 memory channels each at up to 4,000 MT/s; how many are populated was not checked. Host memory is slower to read than GPU memory (how much slower was not measured), but there is five times as much of it as one GPU has. A memory-backed file system (`/dev/shm`, 63 GB) lets processes share memory by name.

**What it makes possible.** The host has room to hold things the GPUs cannot, at the cost of the link to reach them.

**How this was checked.** Sizes read from `free` and `df`. Memory speed was not measured.

**Where to read more.** `lscpu`, `dmidecode` output where readable without root.

(card: `28-host-memory.md`)

### Copy engines beside the compute engine

**What it is.** Each GPU reports two copy engines, separate from the hardware that runs kernels. A copy between host and GPU can proceed while a kernel runs, and a copy in each direction can proceed at the same time.

**What it makes possible.** Moving data on or off a GPU does not have to stop computation on it, and sending does not have to wait for receiving.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes).

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Asynchronous Execution.

(card: `17-copy-engines-beside-the-compute-engine.md`)

### More than one kernel at a time

**What it is.** A GPU can have up to 128 kernels resident at once. Kernels issued on different streams of one process may run at the same time when the first leaves SMs, registers or scratch memory free; otherwise they run one after another. Streams can be given priorities. The two GPUs are fully independent of each other.

**What it makes possible.** A small kernel does not have to wait behind a large one, and a kernel that cannot use the whole chip need not leave the rest idle.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes).

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Asynchronous Execution.

(card: `12-more-than-one-kernel-at-a-time.md`)

## This machine: software

### Compiling a whole sequence of operations as one

**What it is.** The framework can trace a model's sequence of operations and hand it to a compiler as one unit, which may fuse operations, drop intermediate results and generate code specialized to the shapes seen. Separately, a sequence of GPU operations can be captured once and replayed as a graph.

**What it makes possible.** Boundaries between operations are a choice of the code as written, not of the computation.

**How this was checked.** Part of the installed framework (torch 2.14); not exercised here.

**Where to read more.** PyTorch documentation: `torch.compile`, `torch.fx`, CUDA graphs.

(card: `04-compiling-a-whole-sequence-of-operations-as-one.md`)

### The framework underneath

**What it is.** The framework keeps its own pool of GPU memory and hands out pieces without asking the driver each time; the pool's behaviour can be tuned and inspected. New operations can be written in C++ or CUDA and loaded as extensions, built ahead of time or when the program starts. The framework exposes streams, events, pinned host memory and non-blocking copies. Each operation passes through a dispatch layer on the host before any GPU work is issued.

**What it makes possible.** The framework's defaults are one arrangement among several it supports, and anything it lacks can be added beside it.

**How this was checked.** From the installed framework (torch 2.14); not exercised here.

**Where to read more.** PyTorch documentation: CUDA semantics, memory management, C++ extensions.

(card: `10-the-framework-underneath.md`)

### Parallel building blocks

**What it is.** A library of general parallel algorithms for the GPU is installed, in C++ and with a Python interface: sort, scan (running totals), reduce, select the items that meet a condition, partition, remove duplicates, histogram, run-length encode, search, merge.

**What it makes possible.** Problems that look inherently sequential, such as compacting a list or grouping equal items, have well-known parallel forms that are already written.

**How this was checked.** The package is installed (CCCL 13.3); not exercised here.

**Where to read more.** CCCL documentation (Thrust, CUB, `cuda.cccl`).

(card: `06-parallel-building-blocks.md`)

### The operating system: what a user can control

**What it is.** Without administrator rights a user can still: choose which cores a thread or process may run on; choose that a thread's memory is allocated on the half of the host where it first touches it; request large pages for a memory range (the system grants them on request, not by default); lock up to about 15.7 GB of memory so it is never paged out; lower a process's priority; create shared memory. A tool to set core affinity is installed; the usual tool for memory placement is not, though the underlying system calls are available.

**What it makes possible.** Where threads run and where memory sits is left to the system's defaults unless the program says otherwise.

**How this was checked.** Read from `ulimit`, `/sys/kernel/mm/transparent_hugepage/` and the installed tools.

**Where to read more.** `man` pages: `sched_setaffinity`, `mbind`, `madvise`, `mlock`, `taskset`.

(card: `11-the-operating-system-what-a-user-can-control.md`)

### Several levels of language for GPU code

**What it is.** GPU code can be written at different levels, and several are installed here. At thread level: CUDA C++ (compiler 13.0) and Numba (Python compiled to the same thing). At block level: Triton, where one program describes a whole thread block and the compiler arranges the threads. At tile level: NVIDIA's CuTe DSL (the building blocks of its matrix-kernel library, usable from Python) and cuTile (kernels written as operations on whole tiles).

**What it makes possible.** The level sets what the author controls and what the compiler decides. The same kernel can be cheap to write at one level and only reach full speed at another, and pieces written at different levels can live in one program.

**How this was checked.** All import in this environment. cuTile's compiler was not run on this chip.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Writing SIMT Kernels, Writing Tile Kernels; the Triton, Numba, CuTe DSL and cuTile documentation.

(card: `01-several-levels-of-language-for-gpu-code.md`)
