# Inspiration cards

These cards are offered for inspiration only. Nobody chose them for your team, and they are not a
direction or an assignment.

- **Look at the system yourself first:** read the task, profile, find where the time goes. Then
  come back to the cards. Using none of them is fine.
- **The principle cards are general.** They say nothing about this system; applying them is your
  work.
- **The hardware and software cards are high-level pointers, not a reference.** They may be
  incomplete, out of date or wrong for your case, and each says how it was checked. Do your own
  research and verify any detail before you rely on it.

## General principles

### Representation

**The principle.** The same information can be written in many forms. Forms differ in size, and in which operations are cheap on them. The cost of storing, moving and processing follows the form, not the information. A form is usually chosen once, for convenience, and everything afterwards pays for it.

**The waste.** Extra size and extra steps caused by the form, not by the information.

**General directions.**
- **A smaller form:** no larger than the information it holds.
- **A form that makes the frequent operation cheap:** even if rare operations get dearer.
- **Arrange for use:** put next to each other what is used together.
- **Convert once:** at the edge, not repeatedly inside.
- **Several forms:** the same information kept in different forms for different uses.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Arithmetic is easy in Arabic numerals and hard in Roman ones. A phone book is sorted by name because that is how it is searched. A map and a list of turns hold the same route for different uses.

(card: `13-representation.md`)

### Fixed choices

**The principle.** Many decisions (a size, a limit, an order, a method) are made once, in advance, from a guess about conditions. But conditions vary: between inputs, with load, over time. A fixed choice is right for one condition at most and somewhat wrong for the others. The information needed for a better choice often exists at the moment of use and costs little to observe.

**The waste.** The gap between the fixed choice and the best choice for the conditions that actually occur.

**General directions.**
- **Observe, then choose:** look at the conditions before deciding.
- **Prepare several versions:** and select among them at the moment of use.
- **Adjust by feedback:** measure the result and correct the choice continuously.
- **Decide later:** postpone the decision until more is known.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A thermostat replaces a heater that is always on. Traffic lights change their timing with the traffic. A gearbox picks a ratio for the current speed.

(card: `16-fixed-choices.md`)

### Predictability

**The principle.** Waiting for a result is necessary only if the result can't be predicted. If a guess is usually right, and checking a guess costs less than waiting for the answer, work can go ahead on the guess and be corrected in the rare cases it was wrong. The gain is roughly (chance of being right x time saved) - (chance of being wrong x work wasted). It needs three things: a cheap guess, a cheap check, and a way to undo.

**The waste.** Time spent waiting for an answer that was largely known in advance.

**General directions.**
- **Guess and verify:** proceed on the likely answer; check it when the real one arrives.
- **Prepare ahead:** get ready what is likely to be needed next.
- **Follow several possibilities:** and keep the one that turns out right.
- **Improve the odds or lower the price:** a better guess, a cheaper check, or a cheaper undo.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A processor guesses which way a branch will go and runs ahead. A waiter starts a regular's usual order when they walk in. A disk reads the next blocks before they are asked for.

(card: `08-predictability.md`)

### Rework

**The principle.** Some work is done and then thrown away: an attempt fails, a guess is wrong, a result arrives too late or is made invalid, something is retried from the start. Its cost was paid and it contributed nothing. This is often accepted as rare, and rarely measured.

**The waste.** All work whose result is discarded.

**General directions.**
- **Measure it:** how much work is thrown away, and why?
- **Detect the failure earlier:** before more work is built on top of it.
- **Keep what is still valid:** discard only the part that was wrong.
- **Fail less often:** remove the cause of the discarded attempts.
- **Make a failed attempt cheaper:** so that being wrong costs little.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A carpenter measures twice and cuts once. A long calculation saves its progress so a failure doesn't restart it. A factory inspects at each station, not only at the end of the line.

(card: `19-rework.md`)

### Unused results

**The principle.** Work has value only through what uses its output. Follow any output forward: if nothing reads it, or only part of it is read, the unread part bought nothing. This happens because the producer usually doesn't know what the consumer will need, so it produces everything to be safe.

**The waste.** Work whose output nobody uses, and the unused part of outputs that are only partly used.

**General directions.**
- **Produce on demand:** wait until something asks for the output.
- **Produce only the part asked for:** let the request say how much is needed.
- **Tell the producer:** pass the consumer's needs back to where the work is done.
- **Stop early:** once the answer is determined, the rest of the work adds nothing.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A restaurant cooks to order instead of cooking the whole menu. A search stops at the first match when one is enough. A map loads only the area on the screen.

(card: `02-unused-results.md`)

## This machine: hardware

### Telling the cache what to keep

**What it is.** A program can influence the second-level cache instead of leaving it to the default policy. Up to 49.5 MiB can be set aside for data marked to persist. A window of memory (up to 128 MiB) is marked per stream or per graph node, with a ratio that says what fraction of accesses in it get the persisting treatment; accesses can also be marked as streaming (use once, evict first). At the instruction level, a load or store can carry an eviction hint (evict first, evict last, do not cache), and there are instructions to prefetch into the cache, to change the priority of a range, and to discard a range without writing it back.

**What it makes possible.** The program knows which data it will need again and which it will not; the default policy does not. These controls let it pass that knowledge on.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes). The hint instructions were assembled for this chip; not run. The set-aside cannot be changed while the multi-process service is in use.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: L2 Cache Control; PTX ISA reference: `prefetch`, `createpolicy`, `applypriority`, `discard`.

(card: `07-telling-the-cache-what-to-keep.md`)

### Host memory as common ground

**What it is.** Host memory can be pinned (locked in place) and registered with the driver, after which either GPU can copy to and from it directly. The same pinned buffer can serve both GPUs. Memory that already exists, including memory shared between processes, can be registered after the fact, and can be registered read-only. Pinned host memory can also be mapped into a GPU's address space, so a kernel reads or writes it directly, each access crossing the link.

**What it makes possible.** The one place both GPUs can reach is host memory. How that memory is laid out, who writes it and who reads it is open to design.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes).

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Unified and System Memory (page-locked host memory).

(card: `24-host-memory-as-common-ground.md`)

### More than one kernel at a time

**What it is.** A GPU can have up to 128 kernels resident at once. Kernels issued on different streams of one process may run at the same time when the first leaves SMs, registers or scratch memory free; otherwise they run one after another. Streams can be given priorities. The two GPUs are fully independent of each other.

**What it makes possible.** A small kernel does not have to wait behind a large one, and a kernel that cannot use the whole chip need not leave the rest idle.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes).

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Asynchronous Execution.

(card: `12-more-than-one-kernel-at-a-time.md`)

### Memory that lives on both sides

**What it is.** Unified (managed) memory is one address range valid on the host and on the GPU. Pages move to whichever side touches them, and both sides can hold pages at the same time. A program can give hints: where a range should preferably live, that it is mostly read, or that it should be moved now ahead of use. A range may be larger than the GPU's free memory; pages are evicted to the host as needed. Ordinary pageable host memory cannot be read by the GPU directly on this system.

**What it makes possible.** Data need not have one fixed home, and the total can exceed what the GPU holds, at the price of page moves when the wrong side touches a page.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes).

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Unified Memory.

(card: `10-memory-that-lives-on-both-sides.md`)

### Every transfer has a fixed cost

**What it is.** Moving data across a link costs a fixed amount of time that does not depend on the size: a call into the driver, setting up the transfer, and learning that it has finished. Only above some size does the time grow with the number of bytes.

**What it makes possible.** For small transfers the count matters more than the bytes. The size at which the fixed part stops dominating can be measured, and it tells you which regime a given exchange is in.

**How this was checked.** A general property of this kind of link; the fixed cost on this machine is not stated here. Measure it.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Asynchronous Execution.

(card: `23-every-transfer-has-a-fixed-cost.md`)

## This machine: software

### Parallel building blocks

**What it is.** A library of general parallel algorithms for the GPU is installed, in C++ and with a Python interface: sort, scan (running totals), reduce, select the items that meet a condition, partition, remove duplicates, histogram, run-length encode, search, merge.

**What it makes possible.** Problems that look inherently sequential, such as compacting a list or grouping equal items, have well-known parallel forms that are already written.

**How this was checked.** The package is installed (CCCL 13.3); not exercised here.

**Where to read more.** CCCL documentation (Thrust, CUB, `cuda.cccl`).

(card: `06-parallel-building-blocks.md`)

### Ready-made math libraries

**What it is.** NVIDIA's tuned libraries are installed: dense linear algebra (including a lightweight matrix-multiply interface with many formats, and batched forms), sparse linear algebra, matrix multiply with one operand pruned in the fixed pattern the matrix hardware supports, solvers, Fourier transforms, random numbers, the standard deep-learning operations, image and JPEG routines, and reading files directly into GPU memory.

**What it makes possible.** Some of these implement an operation in a way nobody would write by hand, and some expose hardware modes that are otherwise awkward to reach.

**How this was checked.** The packages are installed; which code paths each takes on this chip was not checked.

**Where to read more.** cuBLAS, cuSPARSE, cuSPARSELt, cuSOLVER, cuFFT, cuRAND, cuDNN, NPP, nvJPEG and cuFile documentation.

(card: `05-ready-made-math-libraries.md`)

### Sharing without copying

**What it is.** Several mechanisms let two parties use the same bytes. Host processes can share memory by name (63 GB available in the memory-backed file system). GPU memory can be shared between processes. Libraries in one process can hand each other GPU arrays without a copy through a standard exchange format (DLPack) or a common array interface. Message passing between processes is available with zero-copy for large messages.

**What it makes possible.** A boundary between processes or between libraries does not have to cost a copy.

**How this was checked.** The mechanisms are present in the installed software; not exercised here.

**Where to read more.** DLPack; PyTorch `torch.multiprocessing`; CUDA Programming Guide: Interprocess Communication; ZeroMQ guide.

(card: `08-sharing-without-copying.md`)

### The framework underneath

**What it is.** The framework keeps its own pool of GPU memory and hands out pieces without asking the driver each time; the pool's behaviour can be tuned and inspected. New operations can be written in C++ or CUDA and loaded as extensions, built ahead of time or when the program starts. The framework exposes streams, events, pinned host memory and non-blocking copies. Each operation passes through a dispatch layer on the host before any GPU work is issued.

**What it makes possible.** The framework's defaults are one arrangement among several it supports, and anything it lacks can be added beside it.

**How this was checked.** From the installed framework (torch 2.14); not exercised here.

**Where to read more.** PyTorch documentation: CUDA semantics, memory management, C++ extensions.

(card: `10-the-framework-underneath.md`)

### Ways to observe

**What it is.** Tools that work here without administrator rights: a per-kernel profiler with hardware counters, down to source lines and instructions (Nsight Compute); a timeline tracer for host and GPU activity (Nsight Systems); the framework's own profiler; a GPU debugger; sampling of GPU state (utilization, clocks, power, link speed, memory) through `nvidia-smi` or its library; and the Linux `perf` tool for a user's own processes. On this machine GPU hardware counters may be usable on the first GPU only.

**What it makes possible.** Anything that can be observed can be checked instead of assumed, including what the hardware does underneath the software.

**How this was checked.** The tools are installed and have been used on this machine.

**Where to read more.** Nsight Compute and Nsight Systems documentation; `man perf`; NVML reference.

(card: `13-ways-to-observe.md`)
