# Inspiration cards

These cards are offered for inspiration only. Nobody chose them for your team, and they are not a
direction or an assignment.

- **Look at the system yourself first:** read the task, profile, find where the time goes. Then
  come back to the cards. Using none of them is fine.
- **The principle cards are general.** They say nothing about this system; applying them is your
  work.
- **The hardware and software cards are high-level pointers, not a reference.** They may be
  incomplete, out of date or wrong for your case, and each says how it was checked. Do your own
  research and verify any detail before you rely on it.

## General principles

### Imbalance

**The principle.** A system has several resources: time, memory, capacity to move things, different kinds of processing. They are rarely all scarce at once. Usually one limits the system and the others have room to spare. Most work can be done in more than one way, each using a different mix of resources. Gains come only at the limiting resource; spending the others costs nothing until they run out.

**The waste.** A plentiful resource left unused while a scarce one limits the system.

**General directions.**
- **Find the limit:** which resource is the system actually waiting for?
- **Spend the plentiful to save the scarce:** choose the way of working that leans on what is spare.
- **Trade in either direction:** more memory for less work, or more work for less memory or movement.
- **Check again:** after a change the limit may have moved to another resource.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A table of answers spends space to save time; compression spends time to save space. A slow cooker spends hours to save attention. A factory with idle machines and too few workers reorganizes around the workers.

(card: `14-imbalance.md`)

### Fixed choices

**The principle.** Many decisions (a size, a limit, an order, a method) are made once, in advance, from a guess about conditions. But conditions vary: between inputs, with load, over time. A fixed choice is right for one condition at most and somewhat wrong for the others. The information needed for a better choice often exists at the moment of use and costs little to observe.

**The waste.** The gap between the fixed choice and the best choice for the conditions that actually occur.

**General directions.**
- **Observe, then choose:** look at the conditions before deciding.
- **Prepare several versions:** and select among them at the moment of use.
- **Adjust by feedback:** measure the result and correct the choice continuously.
- **Decide later:** postpone the decision until more is known.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A thermostat replaces a heater that is always on. Traffic lights change their timing with the traffic. A gearbox picks a ratio for the current speed.

(card: `16-fixed-choices.md`)

### Excess guarantees

**The principle.** Every part of a system makes promises to whatever uses it: about order, exactness, freshness, completeness, timing. Each promise costs work to keep. Promises are usually set strong, because strong is safe and the users' real needs weren't known. Often what the users actually rely on is weaker.

The promises a system makes to the outside world are set by its requirements. The promises its parts make to each other are choices.

**The waste.** Work spent keeping a promise nobody relies on.

**General directions.**
- **Ask who relies on it:** for each promise, find what would break without it.
- **Weaken what nobody relies on:** while keeping everything the outside depends on.
- **Keep it only where it matters:** at the points or moments where it is relied on.
- **Keep it more cheaply:** the same promise, delivered by a different mechanism.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Not every letter needs registered mail. A bank need not post each payment the instant it is made. A list need not be fully sorted to find its largest entry.

(card: `18-excess-guarantees.md`)

### Predictability

**The principle.** Waiting for a result is necessary only if the result can't be predicted. If a guess is usually right, and checking a guess costs less than waiting for the answer, work can go ahead on the guess and be corrected in the rare cases it was wrong. The gain is roughly (chance of being right x time saved) - (chance of being wrong x work wasted). It needs three things: a cheap guess, a cheap check, and a way to undo.

**The waste.** Time spent waiting for an answer that was largely known in advance.

**General directions.**
- **Guess and verify:** proceed on the likely answer; check it when the real one arrives.
- **Prepare ahead:** get ready what is likely to be needed next.
- **Follow several possibilities:** and keep the one that turns out right.
- **Improve the odds or lower the price:** a better guess, a cheaper check, or a cheaper undo.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A processor guesses which way a branch will go and runs ahead. A waiter starts a regular's usual order when they walk in. A disk reads the next blocks before they are asked for.

(card: `08-predictability.md`)

### Repetition

**The principle.** Any piece of work is a function: it takes inputs and produces an output. The output contains nothing that wasn't already determined by the inputs. So work is only necessary when the inputs are new. If the inputs were seen before, the output already exists somewhere. If part of the inputs is new, only the part of the output that depends on it needs to be made.

This holds for computing a value, and equally for moving or storing one: sending something to a place that already has it is the same waste.

**The waste.** The work done, minus the work the new information required.

**General directions.**
- **Remember:** keep an output so the work isn't done again. This trades memory for work.
- **Share:** produce an output once and let everyone who needs it use the same copy.
- **Update by difference:** when the inputs change a little, compute only what the change affects.
- **Do it ahead:** if the inputs are known long before the output is needed, the work can be done once, early.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Incremental compilation rebuilds only what a change touched. Rsync sends only the parts of a file the other side lacks. A spreadsheet recomputes only the cells that depend on the edited one.

(card: `01-repetition.md`)

## This machine: hardware

### What this chip does not have

**What it is.** Several features of newer NVIDIA chips are absent on this one: thread-block clusters and scratch memory shared across SMs; the bulk copy unit (TMA) that loads whole tiles with one instruction; moving registers between warps while a kernel runs; add, subtract and multiply directly on bfloat16 values (fused multiply-add on bfloat16 does exist); the block-scaled and 4-bit float formats. Scratch memory per SM is 100 KB, less than on the older data-center chip of the same family (164 KB) and the newer one (228 KB).

**What it makes possible.** Code written for newer chips often assumes these features. Here the same job has to be done another way, or the code falls back to a slower path without saying so. Knowing what is missing tells you which published designs carry over unchanged and which need rethinking.

**How this was checked.** The compiler rejects cluster launch for this chip; the assembler rejects the bulk copy, register reallocation and bfloat16 add; the device reports no cluster launch and no tensor-map access.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Compute Capabilities; PTX ISA reference (the target notes under each instruction).

(card: `03-what-this-chip-does-not-have.md`)

### The paths between the parts

**What it is.** Each GPU connects to the host by its own PCIe link (generation 4, 16 lanes) on its own port. Both links attach to the first processor socket. A link carries about 32 GB/s in each direction at most. A GPU reads its own memory about 30 times faster than that.

**What it makes possible.** Anything that crosses a link is far more expensive per byte than anything that stays on a GPU. How many bytes cross, and how often, is set by how the work is divided.

**How this was checked.** Topology read from `nvidia-smi` and `lspci`. The link rate is the standard's figure, not a measurement.

**Where to read more.** PCI Express specification summaries; `nvidia-smi topo -m`.

(card: `20-the-paths-between-the-parts.md`)

### More than one kernel at a time

**What it is.** A GPU can have up to 128 kernels resident at once. Kernels issued on different streams of one process may run at the same time when the first leaves SMs, registers or scratch memory free; otherwise they run one after another. Streams can be given priorities. The two GPUs are fully independent of each other.

**What it makes possible.** A small kernel does not have to wait behind a large one, and a kernel that cannot use the whole chip need not leave the rest idle.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes).

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Asynchronous Execution.

(card: `12-more-than-one-kernel-at-a-time.md`)

### Every transfer has a fixed cost

**What it is.** Moving data across a link costs a fixed amount of time that does not depend on the size: a call into the driver, setting up the transfer, and learning that it has finished. Only above some size does the time grow with the number of bytes.

**What it makes possible.** For small transfers the count matters more than the bytes. The size at which the fixed part stops dominating can be measured, and it tells you which regime a given exchange is in.

**How this was checked.** A general property of this kind of link; the fixed cost on this machine is not stated here. Measure it.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Asynchronous Execution.

(card: `23-every-transfer-has-a-fixed-cost.md`)

### Memory that lives on both sides

**What it is.** Unified (managed) memory is one address range valid on the host and on the GPU. Pages move to whichever side touches them, and both sides can hold pages at the same time. A program can give hints: where a range should preferably live, that it is mostly read, or that it should be moved now ahead of use. A range may be larger than the GPU's free memory; pages are evicted to the host as needed. Ordinary pageable host memory cannot be read by the GPU directly on this system.

**What it makes possible.** Data need not have one fixed home, and the total can exceed what the GPU holds, at the price of page moves when the wrong side touches a page.

**How this was checked.** Queried from the device on this machine (CUDA driver API device attributes).

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Unified Memory.

(card: `10-memory-that-lives-on-both-sides.md`)

## This machine: software

### Parallel building blocks

**What it is.** A library of general parallel algorithms for the GPU is installed, in C++ and with a Python interface: sort, scan (running totals), reduce, select the items that meet a condition, partition, remove duplicates, histogram, run-length encode, search, merge.

**What it makes possible.** Problems that look inherently sequential, such as compacting a list or grouping equal items, have well-known parallel forms that are already written.

**How this was checked.** The package is installed (CCCL 13.3); not exercised here.

**Where to read more.** CCCL documentation (Thrust, CUB, `cuda.cccl`).

(card: `06-parallel-building-blocks.md`)

### Several levels of language for GPU code

**What it is.** GPU code can be written at different levels, and several are installed here. At thread level: CUDA C++ (compiler 13.0) and Numba (Python compiled to the same thing). At block level: Triton, where one program describes a whole thread block and the compiler arranges the threads. At tile level: NVIDIA's CuTe DSL (the building blocks of its matrix-kernel library, usable from Python) and cuTile (kernels written as operations on whole tiles).

**What it makes possible.** The level sets what the author controls and what the compiler decides. The same kernel can be cheap to write at one level and only reach full speed at another, and pieces written at different levels can live in one program.

**How this was checked.** All import in this environment. cuTile's compiler was not run on this chip.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Writing SIMT Kernels, Writing Tile Kernels; the Triton, Numba, CuTe DSL and cuTile documentation.

(card: `01-several-levels-of-language-for-gpu-code.md`)

### Compiling while the program runs

**What it is.** GPU code does not have to be compiled before the program starts. A program can generate source, compile it in memory (NVRTC), link it with other compiled pieces while still optimizing across them (nvJitLink), and load the result, all while running. The Python kernel languages here work this way already.

**What it makes possible.** At run time the program knows things that were unknown beforehand: sizes, shapes, constants, which case actually occurs. Code compiled then can have them built in.

**How this was checked.** The libraries are installed (NVRTC 13.0, nvJitLink 13.4); not exercised here.

**Where to read more.** NVRTC and nvJitLink documentation; `cuda.core` documentation.

(card: `02-compiling-while-the-program-runs.md`)

### Ready-made math libraries

**What it is.** NVIDIA's tuned libraries are installed: dense linear algebra (including a lightweight matrix-multiply interface with many formats, and batched forms), sparse linear algebra, matrix multiply with one operand pruned in the fixed pattern the matrix hardware supports, solvers, Fourier transforms, random numbers, the standard deep-learning operations, image and JPEG routines, and reading files directly into GPU memory.

**What it makes possible.** Some of these implement an operation in a way nobody would write by hand, and some expose hardware modes that are otherwise awkward to reach.

**How this was checked.** The packages are installed; which code paths each takes on this chip was not checked.

**Where to read more.** cuBLAS, cuSPARSE, cuSPARSELt, cuSOLVER, cuFFT, cuRAND, cuDNN, NPP, nvJPEG and cuFile documentation.

(card: `05-ready-made-math-libraries.md`)

### Compiling a whole sequence of operations as one

**What it is.** The framework can trace a model's sequence of operations and hand it to a compiler as one unit, which may fuse operations, drop intermediate results and generate code specialized to the shapes seen. Separately, a sequence of GPU operations can be captured once and replayed as a graph.

**What it makes possible.** Boundaries between operations are a choice of the code as written, not of the computation.

**How this was checked.** Part of the installed framework (torch 2.14); not exercised here.

**Where to read more.** PyTorch documentation: `torch.compile`, `torch.fx`, CUDA graphs.

(card: `04-compiling-a-whole-sequence-of-operations-as-one.md`)
