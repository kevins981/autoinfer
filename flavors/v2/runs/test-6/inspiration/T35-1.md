# Inspiration cards

These cards are offered for inspiration only. Nobody chose them for your team, and they are not a
direction or an assignment.

- **Look at the system yourself first:** read the task, profile, find where the time goes. Then
  come back to the cards. Using none of them is fine.
- **The principle cards are general.** They say nothing about this system; applying them is your
  work.
- **The hardware and software cards are high-level pointers, not a reference.** They may be
  incomplete, out of date or wrong for your case, and each says how it was checked. Do your own
  research and verify any detail before you rely on it.

## General principles

### Overqualified work

**The principle.** Expensive work is often applied to many candidates, of which only a few matter to the final answer. If a cheap test can tell which candidates cannot matter, the expensive work is needed only for the rest. The final answer still comes from the expensive, exact work, so it stays exact. The cheap test only has to avoid ruling out a candidate that matters.

**The waste.** Exact work spent on candidates that a cheaper test would have eliminated.

**General directions.**
- **Cheap pass first:** then exact work on the survivors only.
- **Use bounds:** a cheap bound can prove a candidate cannot win without evaluating it.
- **Stages of rising cost:** each stage removes candidates before the next, dearer one.
- **Order the tests:** cheapest and most selective first.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Security screens everyone quickly and searches a few by hand. An index rules out most rows before any is read. A chess program drops moves that a bound shows cannot beat the best found so far.

(card: `05-overqualified-work.md`)

### Predictability

**The principle.** Waiting for a result is necessary only if the result can't be predicted. If a guess is usually right, and checking a guess costs less than waiting for the answer, work can go ahead on the guess and be corrected in the rare cases it was wrong. The gain is roughly (chance of being right x time saved) - (chance of being wrong x work wasted). It needs three things: a cheap guess, a cheap check, and a way to undo.

**The waste.** Time spent waiting for an answer that was largely known in advance.

**General directions.**
- **Guess and verify:** proceed on the likely answer; check it when the real one arrives.
- **Prepare ahead:** get ready what is likely to be needed next.
- **Follow several possibilities:** and keep the one that turns out right.
- **Improve the odds or lower the price:** a better guess, a cheaper check, or a cheaper undo.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A processor guesses which way a branch will go and runs ahead. A waiter starts a regular's usual order when they walk in. A disk reads the next blocks before they are asked for.

(card: `08-predictability.md`)

### Per-item overhead

**The principle.** The cost of a piece of work often has two parts: one that grows with its size, and a fixed part paid every time regardless of size (setting up, starting, switching, handing over). When items are small, the fixed part dominates. The fixed part belongs to the act of starting, not to the work itself, so several items can share one.

**The waste.** Fixed costs paid more often than necessary.

**General directions.**
- **Group items:** pay the fixed part once for many.
- **Join steps:** let an item pass through several steps without stopping and restarting between them.
- **Stay set up:** keep the preparation between items instead of tearing it down and rebuilding it.
- **Shrink the fixed part:** if it can't be shared, make it cheaper.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). A bus carries fifty people with one driver. Laundry is done by the load. Goods cross the ocean in containers, not parcel by parcel.

(card: `10-per-item-overhead.md`)

### Skew

**The principle.** Costs are rarely spread evenly. A few cases, items or paths usually account for most of the total. Treating everything the same means designing for an average that hardly occurs: the frequent few get no special care, and the rare many get more than they need.

**The waste.** Uniform treatment of unequal things.

**General directions.**
- **Measure the distribution:** find which few account for most.
- **Make the frequent case cheap:** even if the rare case gets slower.
- **Give the best places to the most used:** the fastest, nearest or shortest.
- **Handle the rare cases apart:** simply, without burdening the common path.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Morse code gives the most common letter a single dot. A supermarket opens an express lane. The most used items in a kitchen sit at hand height.

(card: `15-skew.md`)

### Representation

**The principle.** The same information can be written in many forms. Forms differ in size, and in which operations are cheap on them. The cost of storing, moving and processing follows the form, not the information. A form is usually chosen once, for convenience, and everything afterwards pays for it.

**The waste.** Extra size and extra steps caused by the form, not by the information.

**General directions.**
- **A smaller form:** no larger than the information it holds.
- **A form that makes the frequent operation cheap:** even if rare operations get dearer.
- **Arrange for use:** put next to each other what is used together.
- **Convert once:** at the edge, not repeatedly inside.
- **Several forms:** the same information kept in different forms for different uses.

**Illustrations** (examples only; the best use of this principle may look like none of them). Arithmetic is easy in Arabic numerals and hard in Roman ones. A phone book is sorted by name because that is how it is searched. A map and a list of turns hold the same route for different uses.

(card: `13-representation.md`)

## This machine: hardware

### Four one-way lanes

**What it is.** Each PCIe link carries data in both directions at once at full rate, and each GPU has its own link and two copy engines. So there are four independent one-way paths: host to GPU and GPU to host, for each of the two GPUs.

**What it makes possible.** An exchange between the GPUs does not have to be a sequence of turns. Both GPUs can send and receive at the same moment.

**How this was checked.** Follows from the link type and the device's reported copy engines; simultaneous transfers were not measured here.

**Where to read more.** PCI Express specification summaries; CUDA Programming Guide: Asynchronous Execution.

(card: `22-four-one-way-lanes.md`)

### Work described ahead of time as a graph

**What it is.** A sequence of kernels and copies, with their dependencies, can be recorded once as a graph and then launched as a unit, any number of times. Parameters of individual nodes can be updated without rebuilding the graph. Graphs can contain conditional nodes (if, while, switch) whose condition is written by a kernel on the GPU, so a decision does not have to go back to the host. A kernel can itself launch a graph.

**What it makes possible.** The cost of issuing many small pieces of work is paid once, and some control flow can stay on the GPU.

**How this was checked.** From the documentation; conditional nodes and device-side launch were not tried on this machine.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: CUDA Graphs.

(card: `15-work-described-ahead-of-time-as-a-graph.md`)

### Graphics hardware a compute program can reach

**What it is.** The chip carries hardware built for graphics that compute code can also use. Texture units fetch from a 1-, 2- or 3-dimensional array and blend the neighbouring entries in hardware in a single fetch (the blend weights have limited precision), with built-in handling of out-of-range coordinates; arrays can be up to 131,072 wide in one dimension. The ray-tracing units search a hierarchy of bounding boxes in hardware; they are reached through a separate library.

**What it makes possible.** A table lookup with interpolation, or a search over spatial data, can be done by dedicated hardware that is otherwise idle in a compute program.

**How this was checked.** Texture limits queried from the device. The ray-tracing path was not checked on this machine.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: texture and surface memory; the OptiX documentation.

(card: `18-graphics-hardware-a-compute-program-can-reach.md`)

### Fetching without waiting, inside a kernel

**What it is.** A kernel can start a copy from main memory into its scratch memory and continue working while the copy is in flight. The data does not pass through registers. Barrier objects tell the kernel when a batch of copies has landed, and several batches can be in flight at once (a pipeline).

**What it makes possible.** Fetching the next piece of data can overlap with computing on the current piece, inside a single kernel, instead of the two alternating.

**How this was checked.** Each instruction was assembled for this chip (`ptxas -arch=sm_89`); not run. The bulk form of this copy is not available on this chip.

**Where to read more.** CUDA Programming Guide: Asynchronous Data Copies; Pipelines; Asynchronous Barriers.

(card: `08-fetching-without-waiting-inside-a-kernel.md`)

### Links that slow down when idle

**What it is.** A PCIe link can drop to a lower speed or a low-power state when idle and return to full speed when traffic resumes. On this machine an idle GPU's link was seen reporting generation 1 instead of 4. Coming back to full speed takes time.

**What it makes possible.** The first transfer after a quiet period may not see the same link as a transfer in a steady stream.

**How this was checked.** The idle link speed was read from `nvidia-smi`. The time to return to full speed was not measured.

**Where to read more.** `nvidia-smi -q` (GPU link info); PCI Express power management summaries.

(card: `25-links-that-slow-down-when-idle.md`)

## This machine: software

### The framework underneath

**What it is.** The framework keeps its own pool of GPU memory and hands out pieces without asking the driver each time; the pool's behaviour can be tuned and inspected. New operations can be written in C++ or CUDA and loaded as extensions, built ahead of time or when the program starts. The framework exposes streams, events, pinned host memory and non-blocking copies. Each operation passes through a dispatch layer on the host before any GPU work is issued.

**What it makes possible.** The framework's defaults are one arrangement among several it supports, and anything it lacks can be added beside it.

**How this was checked.** From the installed framework (torch 2.14); not exercised here.

**Where to read more.** PyTorch documentation: CUDA semantics, memory management, C++ extensions.

(card: `10-the-framework-underneath.md`)

### Down to the instructions

**What it is.** The compiler's intermediate language (PTX) can be read, written by hand, and placed inline inside C++ code. The assembler reports registers used and spilled per kernel. The final machine instructions can be disassembled from any compiled kernel, including ones from libraries. The profiler can attribute time to single source lines and instructions.

**What it makes possible.** What a kernel really does is visible, and what the higher-level languages do not expose can still be written.

**How this was checked.** The tools are installed (`ptxas`, `nvdisasm`, `cuobjdump`) and the assembler was used to check the hardware cards.

**Where to read more.** PTX ISA reference; CUDA binary utilities documentation.

(card: `03-down-to-the-instructions.md`)

### The communication library, beyond one operation

**What it is.** The library that moves data between GPUs (NCCL 2.30) offers more than the sum-across-GPUs operation: broadcast, reduce to one GPU, gather from all, reduce-then-scatter, and plain send and receive between two GPUs. Several operations can be grouped and issued together. Buffers can be registered ahead of time. It has a Python interface. Its newest feature, communication started from inside a kernel, needs direct GPU-to-GPU access and so does not apply on this machine.

**What it makes possible.** The exchange pattern between the GPUs is a choice from a larger set than the one in use, and the library is not the only way to move data.

**How this was checked.** The package and its Python interface are installed. Which transport it selects on this machine was not checked here.

**Where to read more.** NCCL user guide and release notes.

(card: `07-the-communication-library-beyond-one-operation.md`)

### The operating system: what a user cannot control

**What it is.** Some conditions are fixed for an ordinary user. The processor frequency policy raises a core's speed only after load appears on it. The system may move a process's memory pages between the two halves of the host on its own. No cores are reserved: any thread can be interrupted or moved to make room for another, and real-time priority is not available. Other people use the machine. The driver version (580) caps the CUDA version at 13.0. The kernel is Linux 5.15.

**What it makes possible.** These are the conditions any design has to work under. A design that depends on a quiet, pinned, fully warmed-up host will not always get one.

**How this was checked.** Read from `/sys` and `/proc` on this machine.

**Where to read more.** Linux kernel documentation: CPU frequency scaling, NUMA balancing, scheduler.

(card: `12-the-operating-system-what-a-user-cannot-control.md`)

### How Python runs

**What it is.** The Python here is 3.12. One thread at a time executes Python code within an interpreter; the others wait for a lock. Native code called from Python can release the lock while it works, and most heavy library calls do. Separate processes each have their own lock, and so can separate interpreters within one process (in this version they can only be created from native code). Starting a thread is cheap and it shares all memory; a process is isolated and shares only what is arranged.

**What it makes possible.** Whether two pieces of host work truly overlap depends on where the lock is held, not on how many threads exist.

**How this was checked.** Version read from the environment. Whether a given library call releases the lock has to be checked case by case.

**Where to read more.** Python documentation: the global interpreter lock, `threading`, `multiprocessing`.

(card: `09-how-python-runs.md`)
